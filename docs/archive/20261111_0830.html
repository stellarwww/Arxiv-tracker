Beyond Modality Preference: Controlling Multimodal Arbitration under Modality Conflict in MLLMs
Authors: Zhaoying Pan, Shenyu Lu, Jun Hui Wong, Junyi Chai, Xiaoqian Wang

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) integrate information across modalities, but become unreliable when those modalities disagree. Under conflicting inputs, MLLMs may favor modalities that are not the most reliable for the task, thus motivating inference-time control of modality arbitration. We identify a small set of attention heads, termed arbitration heads, that causally influence whether predictions follow visual or textual evidence. Building on this, we introduce a lightweight, training-free intervention that biases these heads toward a designated trusted modality at inference time. The identified heads are stable across calibration set sizes and generalize across settings, and steering them enables effective bidirectional control of modality reliance across diverse benchmarks. Our findings identify an effective control mechanism to align MLLM predictions with task-specified modality priorities. The code is available at https://github.com/zhaoyingpan/modality-arbitration.

Abstract
The timeline by which quantum computers can solve breakthrough problems in science depends on improved quantum algorithms as much as on better hardware. Such algorithmic advances are typically achieved through many years of expert human collaboration. In mathematics and computer science, large language models have accelerated progress through automated discovery loops. However, quantum algorithms can only be evaluated by classical computers on restricted or small instances. Here we demonstrate automated discovery of quantum programs beyond these regimes including order-of-magnitude improvements on scientific applications. To achieve this, we build a quantum program library in the Lean theorem prover in which Gemini 4 agents synthesize novel programs against formal problem specifications and verify their correctness and cost. Agent teams collaborate, sharing results and knowledge through wikis and discussing ideas at organized conferences. Our results improve on prior baselines for parallel search (up to 2.55x), quantum arithmetic (290x), and simulations relevant to fusion, photochemistry and drug-metabolism by 11x, 8x and 2.5x respectively. Combining human and artificial intelligence at scale, under the rigor of mathematical proof, lets researchers design algorithms that today's classical and quantum computers cannot test, shortening the path to practical quantum computation.

SWAM: Scaling World Action Models with Cross-Modal Alignment
Authors: Yu Zhou, Xinyuan Xia, Xuankun Yang, Yinghao Yang, Wendi Chen, Bowen Liu, Jianhan Ma, Weicheng Duan, Jun Lv, Cewu Lu, Yanwei Li
First: 2026-11-09T18:55:04+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-09T18:55:04+00:00

Abstract
World action models jointly predict video and actions conditioned on language. Existing studies focus primarily on architectures and training mechanisms, typically treating language-video alignment acquired through video pretraining as an established foundation. However, introducing action learning may impair this alignment. Principled guidance on data and supervision design for maintaining language-video alignment during action learning remains limited. To investigate this, we curate a pool of approximately 47,000 hours of general videos, egocentric videos, UMI demonstrations, and robot demonstrations. Building on this, we systematically study how language-video supervision, language-video-action supervision, and the joint use of these two forms affect WAM performance. In particular, we examine how language annotation quality affects pretrained language-video capabilities, and how action supervision from UMI and robot demonstrations extends these capabilities to language-video-action modeling. To better scale the model, we further conduct detailed studies on joint training recipe and model architecture. Under a fixed training budget, mixing egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations improves control generalization. Model architecture affects how effectively these experiences are used, while scaling pretraining under the selected configuration brings further gains. The resulting recipe, post-trained on RoboTwin Clean demonstrations only, achieves success rates of 88.10% and 61.56% under Easy (Clean-to-Clean) and Hard (Clean-to-Random) conditions, respectively. Our model achieves 40.00% SR across three real-world tasks, compared with 23.33% for the strongest baseline, indicating better generalization under unseen conditions. We release the curated data, training code, and model weights.

Where Does Alignment Emerge and What Does It Mean? A Closer Look at the Platonic Representation Hypothesis

Abstract
The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) suggests that neural networks are converging toward similar representation spaces. As language models become more capable, their representations are aligning with those of vision models. However, recent work has questioned whether this trend continues to hold for newer language models. Moreover, it has been shown that representation similarity metrics can be confounded by model depth and width, requiring appropriate baselines for interpretation. In this work, we first investigate where alignment emerges between unimodal models across model layers and representation dimensions. Next, we find that the PRH capability-alignment trend persists for newer models as long as the captions are sufficiently clean and informative. Finally, we contextualize cross-modal alignment, introducing unimodal and multimodal baselines on various datasets. Our results show that the cross-modal representational alignment of unimodal models can surpass that of explicitly aligned vision-language models, but remains substantially behind the alignment observed for models of the same modality. Code is available at https://github.com/ExplainableML/prh-closer-look.

Mitigating Agentic Deception via Evaluation-Side Partially Observable Games
Authors: Hanxiao Li, Shutong Liu, Yongjia Ma, Jiaming Ji, Juntao Dai, Shuning Zhang, Kejian Shi, Kai Chen, Pu Feng, Yuqing Ma, Qi Dou, Yaodong Yang, Xianglong Liu, Simin Li

Abstract
As large language models become more capable agents, they can exhibit deceptive behaviors, cheating human for higher rewards while appearing helpful. We attribute this failure to evaluation-side partial observability, where models are evaluated mainly from final outcome, without checking the faithfulness of underlying reasoning, tool use, and environmental effects. Prior mitigations address this partial observability by predefined evaluator latent belief space or one-shot monitoring, which do not extend to free-form language space and vulnerable to stronger deceptions that evade simple monitoring. In this work, we formulate deception mitigation as an Evaluation-Side Partially Observable Extensive Form Game (e-PoG), a max-min framework in which an agent maximizes reward, a monitor minimizes the agent's payoff by exposing deception from the full interaction trajectory, and a fixed verifier validates the monitor's claims and converts them into penalties. We define the solution concept as a Subgame-Perfect Oversight Equilibrium (SPOE) between agent and monitor, and prove its existence in finite games. With a perfect verifier, every SPOE is faithful; with an imperfect verifier, the guarantee persists if verifier errors do not reverse the ranking of monitor actions. To achieve faithful SPOE, we derive a two-timescale policy gradient algorithm and enhance the pipeline empirically with NLI verification and deterministic checks. Experiments with Gemma3-4B-IT and Qwen3-4B/8B on Agentic Role-Playing, SearchQA, and WebShop show that e-PoG mitigates multiple emergent deception modes, outperforming the strongest baselines by 26.0% in deception mitigation and by up to 39.8% in task success. These gains persist under weak-to-strong supervision. Code is available at https://github.com/Toyoid/deceptive-agent.

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are powerful but limited by static parametric knowledge that becomes outdated once pretraining ends. Knowledge editing addresses this problem by updating model behavior on target facts without full retraining. In particular, in-context knowledge editing has gained attention because it is training-free and readily applicable to black-box LLMs. Recent reinforcement learning (RL)-based approaches improve over fixed retrieval strategies by adapting prompt construction to the quantity-quality trade-off. Despite initial success, they fail to model the prompt as a structured entity under the distinct and often competing objectives of reliability, generality, and specificity. We propose Multi-Objective In-context Knowledge Editing (MO-IKE), a multi-objective RL algorithm that formulates prompt construction for in-context knowledge editing as a Constrained Markov Decision Process. MO-IKE trains a dynamic retriever to optimize competing objectives in knowledge editing, enabling more balanced and globally coherent prompt construction. Abstract
We introduce OCRia-1, a 4B vision-language model (VLM) for document parsing that excels at preserving table structure, extracting chart data and retaining text formatting. Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) excel at visual interpretation but fail on spatial reasoning tasks that humans solve reliably. Existing benchmarks evaluate these models as black boxes, limiting their ability to identify the underlying causes of lower performance: when a model fails a spatial reasoning task, it remains difficult to ascertain whether the hurdle is perceptual, such as recognizing object boundaries, or cognitive, such as reasoning about occlusion to infer hidden geometry. We introduce Spatial-IQ, a hierarchical diagnostic framework that decomposes object counting in stacked 3D structures into 9 perceptual and cognitive sub-tasks organized by the developmental stages of human spatial cognition, with mental rotation as an additional target probe. Using NVIDIA Isaac Sim, we procedurally generated a diverse dataset of roughly 80,000 stacked 3D structures with per-task ground truth. We evaluate models across three output formats (free-response text, multiple-choice images, and image editing) alongside a human baseline. The Spatial-IQ framework shows that top-performing models often succeed at the target task (object counting) without succeeding on the lower-level sub-tasks intended to support it, and that models differ in how much of these hierarchical chains they preserve, often revealing shortcut behavior that raw target-task accuracy alone would obscure. Finally, we demonstrate that training models with chain-of-thought (CoT) supervision over our hierarchical sub-tasks, combined with reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, significantly improves both spatial consistency across sub-tasks and target-task accuracy, supporting the value of the proposed decomposition as both a diagnostic tool and a training signal.

Abstract

Abstract
Generative models, from diffusion models to large language models, achieve remarkable performance but at a cost in training data orders of magnitude larger than what biological learners require. An alternative paradigm has emerged in which networks are trained to predict their own latent representations of related views or masked regions, as in data2vec and JEPA -- an idea related to predictive-coding accounts of the cortex. Despite strong empirical results, the theoretical understanding of these methods remains limited. Central questions include: by how much does latent prediction actually improve data efficiency? Is there a benefit to stacking such methods into multi-scale hierarchies? We answer both using as data a tractable probabilistic context-free grammar that captures the compositional structure of natural language and images. Such a grammar generates strings of visible tokens by recursively applying production rules along a tree of hidden symbols of depth $L$. For such data, supervised or token-level SSL require a number of samples exponential in $L$ to recover the latent tree; we prove that latent prediction achieves this with a number of samples constant in $L$, up to logarithmic factors. We confirm this bound with (i) a hierarchical clustering algorithm, (ii) an end-to-end neural network whose predictor-clusterer modules predict their own latents at each level via gradient descent, and (iii) the first sample-complexity analysis of data2vec, which we show implicitly performs hierarchical latent prediction. This suggests that explicit stacking such as H-JEPA is largely redundant.

Abstract
Recent work on hallucination detection in large language models has shown that, for a fixed pre-trained model and reasoning task, it is possible to estimate the model's confidence in the correctness of its outputs. Such uncertainty estimates have primarily been used to improve truthfulness by detecting or filtering confabulations. In this work, we ask whether these signals can instead be used more proactively to directly improve the accuracy of model-generated answers. Abstract
Prompt-based continual learning adapts a frozen pre-trained vision transformer by tuning a small set of prompt tokens. Recently, one-prompt methods which share one single prompt across all tasks make this paradigm maximally parameter efficient. The open question is how a shared prompt can keep the knowledge of old classes while being updated for new ones. Existing methods protect old knowledge in parameter space, where they freeze task-specific prompts, expand new components, or sparsely gate which tokens receive gradients. However, this method sacrifices plasticity, yet the drift it still permits in shared tokens can corrupt the prompt's function on past classes. What must be preserved is the prompt's behavior. TEA-Prompt achieves state-of-the-art accuracy with markedly lower forgetting on CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, and CUB-200 with only 0.38M parameters. Abstract
Preference optimization has become a central approach for aligning large language models with human values, but its effectiveness depends heavily on the quality of preference annotations. In practice, preference data is often noisy due to annotation errors and ambiguity. Existing robust preference optimization methods primarily operate at the sequence level, implicitly treating entire responses as uniformly correct or incorrect. However, rejected responses may still contain informative reasoning steps, while preferred responses can include subtle errors. In this work, we propose \textbf{P}reference \textbf{O}ptimization via \textbf{R}obust \textbf{T}oken-level reweighting \textbf{(PORT)}, a fine-grained framework for robust alignment under noisy preferences. PORT performs token-level reweighting using the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) of token log-probabilities, yielding an efficient forward-pass-only proxy to gradient-norm penalization that selectively suppresses corrupted tokens without additional backward-pass computation. We provide theoretical analysis showing that PORT reduces the gradient bias from noisy preference labels by minimizing an upper bound on the bias term. Extensive experiments across diverse noise settings demonstrate that PORT consistently improves robustness and outperforms existing sequence-level baselines. Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit strong zero-shot generalization but remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks. Existing adversarial fine-tuning methods often use pre-trained model guidance to preserve image--text alignment while improving robustness. Our analysis of visual representation drift reveals complementary limitations of two representative forms of guidance. Matching visual features constrains all directions equally, providing broad coverage without semantic emphasis. Matching prediction distributions prioritizes semantically relevant directions, but remains limited by the candidate texts and prediction probabilities. These limitations can hinder preservation of pre-trained image--text relationships and degrade zero-shot performance. We therefore propose Textual Response-Aware Guidance (TRAG), which constrains visual representation drift using the pre-trained text encoder's responses to local variations in input embeddings. TRAG constructs anchor-level constraints from the response directions and magnitudes around semantic anchors, then aggregates them using pre-trained image--anchor relevance, yielding an image-adaptive constraint. Extensive experiments across architectures and tasks demonstrate state-of-the-art clean and robust performance. Further analysis shows that TRAG helps preserve pre-trained image--text relationships.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly mediate how people seek and consume information, including accounts that differ across languages and regions, especially on contested topics. Supporting diverse information seeking requires models to help users navigate competing narratives across languages and information streams. We define six capabilities necessary for diverse multilingual information seeking: three foundational capabilities for narrative inclusion, narrative exclusion, and missing narrative identification; and three higher-level capabilities for presenting diverse narratives, preserving narrative coverage under confirmatory queries, and maintaining information parity across languages. To guide the development of those information seeking capabilities, we introduce \textsc{NarrativeBench}, a benchmark comprising 1,333 news articles in 23 publication languages across 23 contested topics and 460 Reddit-grounded queries. Evaluating 14 LLMs across 24 query languages, we find that current models lack the capabilities needed to reliably navigate diverse narratives, limiting users' control over the perspectives they receive and leaving substantial gaps in exposure to competing accounts even when diversity is explicitly requested. Information access also differs across query framings and languages, risking echo chambers, linguistic filter bubbles, and information disparities that weaken the common ground needed for democratic discourse. \textsc{NarrativeBench} provides a foundation for developing information systems that give users across languages and regions agency to navigate diverse narratives.

Abstract
Deploying large language models in regulated industries requires more than picking the fastest inference engine: it requires evidence that a configuration meets contractual and legal obligations before it serves a single customer request. Abstract
World models have increasingly been applied as a model-based control method for robotics tasks such as manipulation and locomotion. While early world model architectures relied on decoders to prevent mode collapse and learn a predictable latent space, recent works on world models, such as Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture or LeWorldModel, increasingly aim to learn embeddings without a grounding decoder, utilising only a latent forward dynamics predictor and a stabilising entropy term. In this paper, we set out to demonstrate that the further introduction of two additional latent losses to LeWorldModel, a backward dynamics predictor and an inverse dynamics action predictor, enforces further structure on the latent embedding, leading to further gains in both performance and planning efficiency. These are critical properties for improving the applicability of such methods to real-time model-based control of real-world robots. We demonstrate these gains on standard world model benchmarks and two simulated robot manipulation tasks.

Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has advanced the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). However, existing RLVR methods often suffer from exploration inefficiency due to mismatches between problem difficulty and model capability: overly difficult problems hinder reasoning path discovery, while overly simple problems offer little learning signal. Abstract
Direct communication between AI systems relies on natural language as an intermediate layer, incurring encoding/decoding overhead, token cost, and latency. We ask whether internal activation states can instead be transferred causally between different large language model (LLM) architectures via a learned projection, evaluated at three levels: representational similarity, cross-model retrieval from projected states, and end-to-end causal transfer via activation injection during generation. Using four architecturally diverse open-weight models (Qwen2-0.5B, Phi-3-mini, Mistral-7B, FLAN-T5-base), we find that representational alignment in trained models exceeds a random-initialization null baseline and is best captured by a rank-based metric (mutual k-nearest-neighbour alignment), more robust to activation-magnitude outliers than centered kernel alignment (CKA) or Procrustes analysis. A learned projection network retrieves the correct target-model representation from a held-out set well above chance for the three causal decoder-only model pairs (45-50% top-1 accuracy vs. 5% chance) but at chance level for the encoder-based FLAN-T5. Injecting projected activations into a target model during generation produces a statistically significant, pre-registered causal effect on retrieval-based output similarity for only one of the three decoder-only pairs (Qwen2-0.5B to Phi-3-mini: 23.3% vs. 0.0% under negative control, p=0.047, FDR-corrected); the two pairs targeting Mistral-7B show no such effect despite comparable representational alignment at the hidden-state level. We interpret these results as evidence for causal transfer of the representational vehicle, not of meaning, and conclude that end-to-end activation-state transfer between LLMs, as currently implemented, is architecture-dependent rather than universal.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) turn writing into a live exchange between humans and software. We characterize this new medium as a discursive network that treats people and LLMs as equal nodes and tracks how their statements circulate. We define the generation of erroneous information as invalidation (any factual, logical, or structural breach) and show it follows four hazards: drift from truth, self-repair, fresh fabrication, and external detection. We develop a general mathematical model of discursive networks that shows that a network governed only by drift and self-repair stabilizes at a modest error rate. Giving each false claim even a small chance of peer review shifts the system to a truth-dominant state. We operationalize peer review with the open-source \emph{Flaws-of-Others (FOO) algorithm}: a configurable loop in which any set of agents critique one another while a harmonizer merges their verdicts. We identify an ethical transgression, epithesis, that occurs when humans fail to engage in the discursive network. The takeaway is practical and cultural: reliability in this new medium comes not from perfecting single models but from connecting imperfect ones into networks that enforce mutual accountability.

Abstract
Discrete diffusion models admit many token orders, yet most systems rely on confidence-based decoding. Confidence is a strong and efficient heuristic, but it can be myopic because local certainty does not measure a position's effect on terminal quality. We introduce DPRM (Doob h-transform Process Reward Model), a plug-in token-ordering module induced by a terminal-reward-tilted trajectory distribution. The Doob transform converts terminal reward into a process reward for each candidate position. A compact estimator indexed by generation progress, confidence, and optional positional or task state makes this correction reusable across partial states. DPRM changes only token order; the host architecture, denoising objective, supervision, and token sampler remain fixed. We derive the exact reward-tilted law and bound its shortlist and online approximations. Across nine recent open-source hosts spanning language, multimodal generation, and scientific sequences, DPRM improves reasoning, numeric VQA, visual-codebook ordering, and preference-conditioned generation. Relative to confidence, it improves PUMA GSM8K by 14.6%, Omni-Diffusion CLIP-L/14 by 13.2%, Prism voted accuracy from 82.41% to 83.85%, and RealWorldQA numeric/count accuracy by 8.97 points. Matched scientific gains include 8.3% in DCM nonzero recovery, 15.0% in GenMol QED, and 53.3% in SDPO-DNA total utility. Entropy and random-order controls show that the gains do not come from favoring uncertain tokens indiscriminately. Token-order traces and shared-state interventions instead show how terminal reward can correct a locally confident but globally poor choice. Code is available at: https://github.com/DakeBU/DPRM-DLLM.

Abstract
We present, to our knowledge, the first end-to-end FP4 RL post-training, in which both the rollout and training policies, including their forward and backward passes, operate at 4-bit precision. A systematic study reveals that the dominant source of degradation in FP4 RL is not training-side quantization error but rollout activation quantization: outliers stretch the dynamic range so far that a large number of activation values underflow to zero under FP4. The experimental results on Qwen2.5-Math-7B further confirm the recovery benefits of Rollout-ResQ with HiF4. Together, these results establish HiF4 as the enabling format for end-to-end FP4 RL post-training, and Rollout-ResQ as the activation-side mechanism that makes the gap to BF16 closable.

Abstract
A representation that scrambles the true degrees of freedom of the world cannot support reliable planning or compositional generalization. We prove that LeJEPA (alignment plus Gaussian regularization) linearly recovers the world's latent variables from nonlinear observations, a property known as linear identifiability, in a class of worlds where latents evolve under stationary, additive-noise transitions. Our main result is that among all such worlds, the Gaussian is the unique latent distribution for which this guarantee holds. The forward direction rests on a spectral decomposition in which each degree of nonlinearity is strictly penalized by alignment, making the linear map the optimum; the converse rules out every non-Gaussian alternative. We further prove an approximate identifiability result where the guarantee degrades gracefully, and show that linear, orthogonal identifiability enables optimal latent-space planning. We validate the theory with experiments ranging from 2D examples to 4096-dimensional latents, including ablations, pixel-based robotic control, and natural images from ImageNet-X. Our theory turns an empirically successful recipe into a mathematical guarantee, providing the foundation for building World Models that provably recover the structure of the world.

Abstract
Weight-space programming sets every weight of a feed-forward network by hand, from an explicit specification of the desired computation, with no gradient descent and no training data anywhere in the construction. We ask how far a large language model can carry this practice, zero-shot, across games of deliberately increasing difficulty. We design a four-rung ladder (Nim, tic-tac-toe, two sizes of Connect Four, and a chess endgame) spanning four axes of difficulty, and build a second, independent network at each rung, using a structurally unrelated game at matched difficulty, to separate what a rung's finding says about the difficulty level from what it says about the one game used to measure it. Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have made significant progress in visual understanding, but precise 3D spatial reasoning integrated with physical environment remains difficult. Furniture assembly requires not only recovering step-level operations from diagrammatic manuals, but also translating semantic attachment relations into 6D pose updates that enable parts to physically interact with the environment and previously assembled components. To study this problem, we propose AssemState, a zero-shot framework for manual and physical-state-guided furniture assembly. It firstly employs anchor-guided boundary assembly states to decompose manual pages into single-part operations and recover an assembly-tree. Then, it uses iterative after-state feedback refinement to guide successive (SE(3)) updates and corrections, and validates their physical plausibility through simulation-based release tests. Experiments show that compared with the strongest prior baseline, AssemState improves F1 from 38.58\% to 62.80\% and Tree Exact Match from 28.24\% to 53.92\% for assembly-tree recovery. On 243 independently evaluated part-level operations, our proposed iterative refinement improves judge-accepted operations from 0 to 5.3\% and reduces mean Chamfer distance from 5.4111 to 1.7744. However, visually plausible candidate poses may still suffer from collision, floating, mirror-orientation errors, incomplete seating, and wrong-side attachment. These results show that AssemState improves operation-structure recovery and selected local pose metrics, while MLLMs remain limited for spatial relationship reasoning.

Abstract
Large Language Model (LLM) code agents increasingly resolve repository-level issues by iteratively editing code, invoking tools, and validating candidate patches. In these workflows, agents often write tests on the fly, but the value of this behavior remains unclear. This raises a central question: how does agent-written testing relate to task outcomes and resource use during repository-level issue resolution? To better understand the role of agent-written tests, we analyze 5,000 recorded agent runs produced by six strong LLMs on SWE-bench Verified using the lightweight mini-SWE-agent scaffold. Our results show that test writing is common for most models. GPT-5.2 is an exception: it rarely writes new test files while achieving a resolution rate close to the top-ranked model. Agents start or stop writing test files as requested on 36.8%-93.2% of tasks. Similar resolution totals can therefore hide substantial differences in how agents test and how many resources they use.

Abstract
As vision-language models (VLMs) move toward interpreting medical images directly, their outputs increasingly include not just a label but a stated confidence and a written justification. Yet the field still predominantly evaluates VLMs using just accuracy. We evaluate the coherence between answer, confidence, and explanation across four VLMs on breast density and BI-RADS categorization, using two mammography datasets. We perform controlled perturbations of the question, multiple choice option orders, and the images and assess changes in the three outputs. Our results suggest confidence does not decrease even as the mammography image is changed. Shuffling the options also changes most VLMs' answers. Abstract
Vision language models (VLMs) encode substantial expert knowledge within their weights, yet their direct application to specialised classification domains such as benthic imagery remains limited. Meanwhile, vision transformers trained on these tasks rely predominantly on low-level visual latents (colours, textures, and shapes) that lack the higher-level reasoning humans employ for identification. We propose Guided Hierarchical Multi-Label (GHiML) learning, a pipeline that extracts conceptual latent variables from a VLM conditioned on training data, generates sample-relevant captions grounded in those variables, and fuses the resulting textual embeddings with image embeddings via cross-attention and a per-node gated router. We also introduce the inverted HML score ($d_{\mathrm{inv}}$), a similarity score that evaluates hierarchical multi-label predictions through graph traversal distance and a cardinality penalty, providing an interpretable alternative to existing measures, which are insensitive to realistic degradations in sparse, deep hierarchies. Experiments on BenthicNet, FathomNet and iNaturalist show that the router improves macro $F_1$ over the vision-only baseline by up to 6.7 pp and $d_{\mathrm{inv}}$ by up to 21.1 pp on in-domain benthic data, with gains persisting under distribution shift. Our results support the hypothesis that VLMs contain exploitable specialised knowledge that, when appropriately elicited, can augment classification in domains where direct visual learning is insufficient.

Abstract
Video world models conditioned on an input video make it possible to explore real, recorded dynamic scenes, enabling re-shooting of footage, free-viewpoint replay, and real-to-sim environments for embodied agents. Such uses require the generated rollout to stay faithful to the recorded world, yet existing benchmarks start from a single image or a text prompt, and video-conditioned generation is evaluated mainly on perceptual quality and camera following. These evaluations do not directly assess whether exploration preserves subject identity, event progression, and scene consistency upon revisiting a location. We introduce WanderBench, a benchmark for exploring dynamic worlds from video. It comprises 745 single-shot source videos of real and AI-generated content in three difficulty tiers (3-25 s), paired with camera routes given as keyboard actions, and evaluates six dimensions with 24 human-validated sub-metrics. Benchmarking eight models, we find that as clips grow longer, models keep the static scene and visual quality but lose camera control and the source dynamics, and that none keeps revisited places consistent. We further propose WanderLand, a training-free framework that conditions each frame of a frozen model on the same-instant source and, on revisits, on its own earlier static content. WanderLand improves five models on every dimension, by up to 8.3 points in source-dynamics fidelity.

Abstract
Language agents increasingly require persistent worlds in which they can act, remember, and learn. Existing approaches sit at two extremes: conventional web frameworks provide reliable but fixed contexts backed by databases, while fully generative world models aim for unlimited environments, but the world is constructed primarily through generation, making it harder to maintain a fixed, deterministic global framework and reducing controllability. In this work, we introduce the Web World Model (WWM), a middle ground where world state and ``physics'' are implemented in ordinary web code to ensure logical consistency, while large language models generate context, narratives, and high-level decisions on top of this structured latent state. We build a suite of WWMs on a realistic web stack, including an infinite travel atlas grounded in real geography, fictional galaxy explorers, web-scale encyclopedic and narrative worlds, and simulation- and game-like environments. Across these systems, we identify practical design principles for WWMs: separating code-defined rules from model-driven imagination, representing latent state as typed web interfaces, and utilizing deterministic generation to achieve unlimited but structured exploration. Our results suggest that web stacks themselves can serve as a scalable substrate for world models, enabling controllable yet open-ended environments.
