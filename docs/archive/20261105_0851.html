GUILD: Guided Iterative Data Collection for Imitation Learning
Authors: Shivin Dass, Kevin Nguyen, Rohan Yelandur, Junhong Xu, Roberto Martín-Martín
First: 2026-11-03T18:57:35+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:57:35+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Imitation learning policies in robotics are trained on datasets collected through costly real-world teleoperation. Often, the data-collection process is unguided, relying on the teleoperator's intuition, resulting in data that contributes little to overall policy performance. Recent work has shown that curating such unguided datasets can substantially improve policy performance, but these approaches operate by filtering existing datasets after collection and therefore do not prevent the acquisition of uninformative data. In this work, we propose GUILD, a novel Guided Data Collection (GDC) method that actively reasons about which data should be collected to maximize downstream policy performance. We introduce an iterative framework that alternates between 1) task proposal and data collection and 2) data evaluation to close the loop. For task proposal, we leverage semantic priors from vision-language models to identify relevant environments, object configurations, and goals for data collection. However, since semantic relevance does not imply policy improvement, we perform data evaluation. By leveraging predictive data attribution we approximate how the collected trajectories influence policy performance on a target deployment setting. This signal is then used as feedback to guide subsequent data collection, closing the data collection loop. We evaluate our approach in simulation and real robot settings, demonstrating that guided data collection substantially improves sample efficiency and deployment performance compared to passive data collection strategies, multiple baselines and experienced roboticists. Adversarial Speculative Reasoning Improves Long Horizon Agents
Authors: Hongyin Luo, Wei Fang, Lijie Yang, Owen Stepan, Daniel Suit, Jeff Krapf, Dana Wensberg, Jack O'Brien
First: 2026-11-03T18:45:23+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:45:23+00:00

Abstract
Speculative decoding has been treated purely as a systems optimization that speeds up large language model decoding without changing the model behavior. Under this view, the growing effort to train better draft models has been focused solely on acceptance rate. We argue that this view does not hold true for long-horizon agent reasoning. Measuring frontier open-weight models on complex agentic tasks with different speculative decoding approaches, we found that numerical noise grows within verification windows, and the draft acceptance results of the thinking process can change the output distribution of the model after 30 steps, thus noticeably influencing the reasoning accuracy of long-horizon agents in random directions. In this work, we improve the reasoning capability of target models by controlling such noise. While we cannot change the overall token distribution verified by the target model, we can encode critical reasoning with highest precision by training the draft model to be rejected. We identify these points in agent traces and train the draft model to favor adversarial preference. At inference the trained draft and the target part ways precisely at these decision points, verification restarts, so that critical thinking tokens are sampled from the target model and encoded under its least noisy conditions. This approach makes critical reasoning better indexed and more durable over a long horizon. On complex agentic coding benchmarks, our approach raises end-to-end success rate with no change to the target model or its output distribution. Our results recast the speculative decoding mechanism from a pure accelerator into an active allocator for context precision that improves the accuracy of long-horizon agents by training 0.06% to 0.28% of hosted parameters.

Pretraining Knows What, Simulation Teaches How: Compositional Generalization for Agentic Robotics
Authors: Benjamin Pikus, Daniel Saunders, Mircea Mironenco, Salman Mohammadi, Mathew Mathew, Sirou Zhu, Divya Makkar, Xuhua Huang, Qinbo Li, Supreeth Narasimhaswamy, Siyuan Cen, Dejia Xu, Shyamal Buch, Thomas Neff

Abstract
Frontier models such as GPT-6 Astra now directly control robots and can generalize better than vision-language-action models (VLAs). We argue that this ability could arise from two distinct, composable skills: semantic reasoning, to understand what a task asks for in a scene, and motion control, to move the robot to do it. We show that small open VLMs are already capable of the first and mainly lack the second. We introduce Zero-real Agent Policy (ZAP), a recipe that teaches this missing motion skill, entirely in simulation on simple primitive tasks and from the model's own successes, with no frontier teacher or distillation and no real-world robot data. ZAP reaches 96.7% success on real-world tasks, matching GPT-6 Astra in our harness while running 3x faster and 17x cheaper, and 74.0% on a bimanual adaptation of RoboLab-120, on par with GPT-6 Astra's 72.2%. ZAP also generalizes to new objects, goals, and strategies, and in simulation to a robot embodiment on which it never trained.

Abstract
The Large Language Model from Power Law Decoder Representations (PLDR-LLM) and its attention, Power Law Graph Attention (PLGA), replace the fixed bilinear form of scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) with a learned, input-generated bilinear operator $G_{\mathrm{LM}}$, built from a positive tensor $A_{\mathrm{LM}}$ by elementwise power laws. The architecture is fully specified, verified against pinned reference releases; claims are labeled theorem, conditional theorem, measurement, or conjecture. Unconditionally: PLGA contains SDPA exactly at $G_{\mathrm{LM}}=I$; $A_{\mathrm{LM}}$ and $A_{P}$ are strictly entrywise positive, with Perron-Frobenius structure on $A_{\mathrm{LM}}$; the DAG regularizer has the NOTEARS walk-counting form and positivity obstructs exact acyclicity; and, under nonresonance (satisfied by standard rotary frequencies), a commutant criterion identifies which operators preserve relative-position dependence. An inference-collapse theorem: exact input invariance of the head score operators $G_{\mathrm{LM}}$ collapses inference to generalized SDPA with a constant operator per head. Measured invariance: relative fluctuations of $10^{-6}$ and below; perturbation bounds quantify but do not certify cached inference; the assembled proxy misses the decoding margin. A conditional three-stage mechanism (rotary twirl, concentration, row-map contraction) is measured on a released checkpoint. Blockwise training and scoring under the global Gram are stated with explicit target exposure; on tested samples, block and sequential scoring select identical answers and agree on the published TruthfulQA probability-mass metric within $5\times10^{-5}$ per item. Self-organized criticality enters as a phenomenological framework with an intrinsic order parameter; open claims become falsifiable conjectures. Selected proof cores are machine-checked in Lean 4.

Knowingly Wrong: Training LLMs Against Their Own Beliefs can Induce Misalignment
Authors: Ariana M. Villegas-Suarez, Guido Freire, Sohan Venkatesh, Bartosz Cywiński
First: 2026-11-03T18:02:21+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:02:21+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Fine-tuning a large language model (LLM) on harmful data in a narrow domain, such as bad medical advice, can make it broadly misaligned. How realistic this risk is remains unclear, since models are very rarely fine-tuned on explicitly harmful data in practice. In this work, we show that fine-tuning on data the model believes to be false but that has no harmful content, can also induce broad misalignment. Across mathematics, trivia, cooking and sports, the more strongly the model disbelieves the answers it is trained on, the more misaligned it becomes. In mathematics, misaligned answers rise from 4% to over 60% as the training answers become more wrong, with little loss of general capability. After fine-tuning, the model states the wrong answers but its multiple-choice probabilities still prefer the true ones, which suggests that misalignment comes from this internal inconsistency. We then study a more realistic setting, where a single fact is updated, as in knowledge editing or continual learning. The same relationship holds and the more the model still believes the true fact after training on the false one, the more misaligned it becomes. When we frame the same false fact as part of a coherent story, the model comes to believe it more deeply and becomes less misaligned than when we instruct it to assert the false fact. Content filters and persona vectors cannot tell these two framings apart, even though they lead to different levels of misalignment. These results suggest a risk for model updates, where training a model that does not adopt the new fact as true can induce misalignment even when the data looks harmless.

Abstract
Evaluating whether a large language model (LLM) genuinely knows a concept remains a fundamental problem, since behavioral metrics can be fooled by suppression: a model that refuses to answer may still harbor recoverable knowledge. The framework measures the geometric coherence of \textit{anon-vectors}, the differences in hidden states between a concept-specific query and its anonymized paraphrase, and shows that genuine knowledge forms a tight, low-entropy cluster while ignorance forces geometric fragmentation. Across ten instruction-tuned LLMs from three model families, all fragmentation metrics reliably separate known from unknown concepts, and post-unlearning, fragmentation geometry correlates with adversarial recovery across four attack types and five unlearning algorithms, though the direction of that relationship is shown to depend on attack methodology. Furthermore, a layer-resolved analysis reveals that different adversarial mechanisms exploit this geometry at varying depths, with high attack-specific correlations that are invisible to behavioral evaluation. Together, these results constitute a single-phase, attack-free proxy for unlearning robustness that captures aspects of parametric knowledge inaccessible to output-level metrics. Abstract
Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) aims to retrieve a target image given a reference image and a modification text. Although recent approaches leveraging Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have shown significant progress, we argue that the MLLM's compositional understanding remains underutilized. This stems from directly mapping composed queries into a retrieval embedding via contrastive supervision alone, without explicitly resolving the intended visual modifications. In this work, we introduce Factorized MLLM Embeddings (FactEmbed), which decouples compositional query comprehension from building discriminative embeddings. Rather than directly projecting the composed query into a single embedding without internal reasoning, FactEmbed employs a set of factorized latents that simulate the intended visual modifications via an intermediate target imagination objective. This process encourages the latents to ground into target semantics before forming discriminative representations for retrieval. Subsequently, to propagate these factorized representations throughout the model for retrieval, we optimize a factor-level contrastive loss while distilling the decomposed representations into a deeper attention layer of the MLLM. Finally, for efficient inference, we retain the [EOS] token to establish a global-level embedding on top of these factorized latents, while we exploit the mean-pooled factorized latents to rerank a short candidate list at negligible cost, as both are produced by the same forward pass. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that FactEmbed outperforms prior works across CIR benchmarks.

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) allow social scientists to analyze video at scale, but researchers must decide which parts of a video to provide as input. We use the term representation validity to examine how text, audio, video, and combined inputs affect measurement and subsequent analysis. We compare input representations across six open-weight MLLMs and three human-annotated video datasets. No representation consistently performs best. On seven news-related constructs, native-video performance varies little across models (macro-F1 = .70-.77), while extracted-text performance ranges from .52 to .80. Three models perform better with text and three with video. Combining inputs rarely improves performance. Higher classification accuracy also does not reliably produce estimates closer to those based on human codes. The highest-F1 representation best reproduces human-coded regression coefficients in 17 of 42 comparisons. Even when each model uses its highest-F1 deployable representation, 45% of sample-prevalence estimates differ from the human-coded benchmark by more than ten percentage points. Input representation therefore requires validation for both the construct being measured and the quantity estimated from the resulting labels.

Abstract
OperAItor, an open-source framework for developing and evaluating large language model agents for supervisory fault management in chemical processes. The framework is demonstrated on a dynamic reactor-distillation process representing isopropanol dehydrogenation, with physically based thermodynamics and modified reaction kinetics to permit liquid-phase operation. The process is represented by a 113-variable differential-algebraic model and operates at a nominal feed rate of 100kmol/h, producing approximately 4t/h of acetone. The simulated plant includes 8 PI control loops, 22 process alarms, 5 duty-standby pump trains, and 11 independent safety instrumented functions. The agent architecture integrates multivariate statistical process monitoring, fault diagnosis, knowledge-graph retrieval, action planning, and supervisory action generation. Five action-validation configurations are investigated, comprising operation without validation, an LLM-based judge, digital-twin simulation, and deterministic admissibility checks with and without consequence projection. The framework is evaluated using four LLMs across 20 scenarios involving single and multiple simultaneous faults. Without agent intervention, 18 scenarios result in plant stops, corresponding to an economic loss of $399,000 relative to fault-free operation under the assumed benchmark economics. For GPT-5 nano, Qwen3 8B, and Mistral 7B, validated agent configurations reduce the number of stops to 5-8 and recover $265,000 of the loss. The study provides a reproducible benchmark for comparing agent architectures, language models, and action-validation methods for supervisory fault management in chemical processes.

Abstract
Scaling vision-language foundation models has significantly advanced generalist robot manipulation, but the increased model scale introduces computational latency that degrades real-time physical reactivity. Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architectures execute high-level semantic reasoning and action ex- ecution sequentially within the primary control loop, creating a fundamental tension between semantic-reasoning capability and robot agility. To address this, we propose Asynchronous Robot Policy (ARP), an architectural framework that decouples semantic reasoning from low-level action generation using asynchronous processes operating at independent frequencies. In ARP, a high-capacity Vision-Language Model (VLM) asynchronously updates a semantic memory at low fre- quency, while a lightweight Vision-Action Expert, conditioned on this semantic memory, generates robot actions at a much higher control frequency. Such a formulation, enables having a fast perception-action loop without compromising on the semantic capability. To overcome asynchronous memory staleness, ARP trains the vision-action expert on the semantic staleness and execution delay, with temporally mismatched observation across processes. On Franka manipulation tasks, ARP achieves an 18% higher success rate and 20% less time need for execution in quasi-static setups, while 3-5 times baseline performance in dynamic simulation environments. Furthermore, ARP enables operating the VLM at 10 times lower than its nominal frequency with no significant performance loss making it much more efficient than the baseline. These results highlight the architectural advantages of modeling robot policies as asynchronous processes, enabling real-world reactive deployment without sacrificing the scale of foundation models.

Abstract

Abstract
The growing energy requirements of large language model inference highlight the need for datacenters to support safe grid operation by providing power demand flexibility, while maintaining service for interactive workloads. This work investigates query routing as a mechanism to enable such capability by dynamically allocating queries across different language model instances without deferring requests. We develop a general analytical framework that connects query arrival rates and sequence length distributions to token iteration time, request residence time, serving capacity, and power consumption of model instances. Leveraging this framework, we characterize the feasible query routing allocations and the corresponding power demand flexibilities, enabling the selection of routing strategies to achieve desired power consumption behavior. We validate our framework across four GPU platforms on language models ranging from 1B to 70B parameters, obtaining 2.8% mean absolute percentage error in estimating achievable power flexibility. We evaluate our approach on a real trace-driven simulation of a 10,000 H100 GPUs datacenter, exhibiting flexibility up to 16% of total power consumption, with 90% of the desired power reduction achieved within approximately 10 seconds. These results establish query routing as an effective mechanism for datacenter power demand flexibility and demonstrate the broader applicability of our proposed framework to datacenter level planning and orchestration.

Abstract

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that negotiate, coordinate, and make decisions on behalf of human users. Understanding how they cooperate is therefore important for AI governance and machine behaviour research. We examine two factors: payoff magnitude and interaction language. Classical game theory predicts that multiplying all payoffs by a positive constant should not change behaviour because the underlying strategic incentives remain unchanged. We test this prediction using five widely adopted commercial LLMs playing an iterated prisoner's dilemma across ten payoff scales and five languages (English, French, Vietnamese, Chinese and Arabic). Contrary to theoretical expectations, changing only the numerical scale of payoffs substantially alters cooperation. Models respond differently, often in opposite directions, meaning that averages across models can conceal important behavioural heterogeneity. Sensitivity to payoff scale also depends strongly on prompt language. Moreover, payoff scale modifies the effects of persona instructions: at sub-unit stakes, where no payoff shown exceeds 1, all five models cooperate more under a selfish than a cooperative persona, whereas Claude and Gemini reverse this pattern at larger scales. These findings show that cooperation measures depend critically on payoff scale and language and should not be treated as fixed model traits.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being used to turn citizens' opinions into collective proposals. These proposals can make assignments: who should act, what must be done, how much one should spend and by when. However, if the assignments change when the same opinions are processed again, publishing a single proposal can hide alternatives that participants never get to see. We examined this issue in four LLM mediators (three open-weight and one commercial) using opinions from DeliberationBank and Polis. For each task, a mediator drafted a proposal from thirty opinions five times, varying either the random seed or the order of the opinions. In 17% of five-run sets from the open-weight mediators and 20% from the commercial one, some runs contained assignments while others did not. Reordering the opinions changed the assignments almost as often as replacing them with opinions from different citizens. Three human annotators confirmed that the detected differences between runs were real, although the LLM judge overstated how often they occurred. Substantial variation remained under four safeguards: temperature zero, a commercial mediator, draft selection, and draft merging. The mediator's objective also shaped representation: prompts seeking agreement covered minority positions much less often than prompts asking to describe differences. A single proposal can therefore obscure choices in both the assignments it proposes and the views it excludes. We propose audits that make alternative assignments visible and disclose the objective behind each proposal.

Abstract
High-fidelity photovoltaic (PV) module mapping requires complete instance recovery and accurate delineation. Vision foundation models offer zero-shot segmentation, but converting incomplete and inconsistent predictions across views into a complete, geometrically accurate map of individual modules remains challenging. We propose the Multi-View PV Module Mapper (MV-PVMapper), a training-free framework coupling vision foundation models with unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) photogrammetry. First, Segment Anything Model 3 (SAM 3) generates candidates from original images using text prompts. Their contours are then transferred into object space through per-module slope correction to limit distortion from surface-model noise, and verified against representative module dimensions to guide cross-view instance selection. Finally, accepted instances return to all covering images as box prompts for SAM~3, supplementing text prompts with spatial cues for iterative segmentation, verification and fusion. On five-direction surveys of three operational rooftop PV plants at approximately 1 cm nadir ground sampling distance, two feedback iterations recover 7,710 of 7,711 reference modules with no false positives at an intersection-over-union (IoU) matching threshold of 0.5. Macro-averaged area precision and recall reach 99.00 % and 98.52 %, respectively. Averaged across sites, the aggregated Jaccard index reaches 97.55 %, compared with 60.73 % and 41.24 % for orthomosaic segmentation and multi-view pixel-voting baselines, respectively; average precision at the stricter IoU threshold of 0.95 reaches 95.67 %. Ablation studies show that module-size constraints are critical for instance separation, while slope correction and feedback to all covering views improve boundary accuracy. These results are achieved without task-specific image annotation or segmentation-model weight updates.

Abstract
Video diffusion alignment has been heavily relied on scalar rewards. These rewards are typically derived from learned reward models in human preference datasets, requiring additional training and extensive collection. Moreover, scalar rewards provide coarse, global supervision, offering limited prompt-generation mismatch credit assignment and making models prone to reward exploitation and unstable optimization. We propose Diffusion-DRF, a free, rich, and differentiable reward framework for video diffusion fine-tuning. Diffusion-DRF employs a frozen, off-the-shelf Vision-Language Model (VLM) as the critic, eliminating the need for reward model training. Instead of relying on a single scalar reward, it decomposes each user prompt into multi-dimensional questions with freeform dense VQA explanation queries, yielding information-rich feedback. By direct differentiable optimization over this rich feedback, Diffusion-DRF achieves stable reward-based tuning without preference datasets collection. Diffusion-DRF achieves significant gains both quantitatively and qualitatively, outperforming state-of-the-art Flow-GRPO by 4.74% in overall performance on unseen VBench-2.0.

Abstract
Speech large language models retain audio history to support multi-turn conversations. Yet remembering an event does not ensure that its information is used only when relevant. We investigate why irrelevant historical audio changes answers even when the queried event remains represented. Controlled experiments on MOSS-Audio and MiMo-Audio show that historical values direct errors toward specific competing answers, beyond a generic effect of context length. Causal interventions distinguish two paths. In open-ended question answering, most history-value errors disappear when subsequent computation cannot directly access the irrelevant source. In multiple-choice answering, many errors persist: early historical influence is carried by question-candidate states even after direct source access is blocked. Low-dimensional interventions further separate source-role information from answer-value information. Their effects peak at different depths, and changing the source-role signal does not eliminate the causal influence of competing values. On naturally occurring errors, strengthening the queried role and counteracting the historical value each improve answers; their joint intervention gives the greatest recovery. These findings support a failure of downstream evidence use beyond source identification alone: source-role information can influence a decision without exclusively controlling which historical values affect it. Reliable conversational memory therefore requires not only retention and retrieval, but also control over how retained information enters answer computation.

Abstract
Test-time adaptation (TTA) enhances the zero-shot robustness under distribution shifts by leveraging unlabeled test data during inference. Despite notable advances, several challenges still limit its broader applicability. First, most methods rely on backpropagation or iterative optimization, which limits scalability and hinders real-time deployment. Second, they lack explicit modeling of class-conditional feature distributions. Abstract
Although existing candidate trajectory evaluators have substantially improved end-to-end planning accuracy, their underlying mechanism remains suboptimal. Model-based evaluators typically make scoring future-aware by explicitly predicting candidate-conditioned future states over the planning horizon, which we term rollout. However, rollout incurs high inference cost and accumulates prediction errors. We argue that planning does not require reconstructing a full future scene for each candidate, but only future-relevant information for reliable comparison. We therefore propose RFWorld, a Rollout-Free World model for end-to-end trajectory evaluation. RFWorld constructs a shared scene memory and shapes it into a future-readable representation via lightweight training-time temporal readout with semantic supervision, keeping training overhead low. At inference time, candidate trajectories directly query the shared memory via sparse spatial readout for scoring, reducing computation by roughly the planning horizon times the number of candidate trajectories, while avoiding recursive rollout errors. Experiments on NAVSIM and Bench2Drive show that RFWorld learns a more planning-aligned world representation and achieves state-of-the-art performance.

Abstract
Multimodal discrete diffusion language models (dLLMs) generate responses through iterative mask prediction and offer a promising alternative to autoregressive large vision-language models. However, they still suffer from hallucination, producing outputs that are plausible under language priors but insufficiently grounded in the image. We argue that hallucination in multimodal dLLMs is not only a token prediction problem, but also a premature commitment problem: during iterative denoising, some masked positions are unmasked too early because their confidence is driven more by language priors than by visual evidence, and these early commitments can bias later generation. To address this issue, we propose Visual Gain Ordering (VIGOR), a training-free and plug-and-play inference strategy that reorders masked positions according to counterfactual visual evidence. At each denoising step, VIGOR compares the confidence of each candidate token under the original image-conditioned pass and an additional visual-ablation pass, and prioritizes positions whose predictions are more strongly supported by the image. Experiments on LLaDA-V and Lumina-DiMOO show that VIGOR consistently reduces hallucination and improves multimodal reasoning performance across benchmarks. Code is available at https://github.com/THU-BPM/Vigor.

Venue: NeurIPS 2026 spotlight

Abstract
Frontier AI systems are increasingly natively multimodal, jointly pretrained on multiple modalities, such as text, vision, and audio. However, the scientific understanding of the multimodal data mixtures used to pretrain these systems --- how much of each modality to mix, and at what potential interference cost to the others --- is largely absent or gatekept. In this work, we derive data mixing scaling laws for three modalities: text, vision, and audio. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) increasingly adopt Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, as MoEs scale efficiently and partially limit cross-modal interference. We therefore use MoEs to conduct 268 experiments ranging from 457M to 8.3B parameters, on up to 150 billion tokens. Our work leads to multiple actionable insights into multimodal data mixing for training MLLMs. First, compute optima are modality-specific: audio modality is model-heavy, favoring scaling parameters over tokens, while text and vision require balanced scaling of model size and data. Second, repeating vision or audio data beyond 2x yields negligible benefits. Finally, cross-modal interactions are highly asymmetric: vision strongly interferes with audio, whereas audio's interference with learning text and vision is negligible. Overall, we provide a principled foundation for understanding the multimodal data mixtures needed to train frontier MLLMs.

Abstract
Pretrained video generation models distill internet-scale motion priors into robot policies, but full video is a redundant signal for action prediction. We argue that dense 3D motion is the medium through which video pretraining best transfers to robot actions, and that predicting motion rather than video affords better sample-efficiency for action prediction. FloMo is a 5B video-generation model fine-tuned to predict 3D scene-flow and action tokens. Rendering scene flow as RGB and encoding it into the same latent space as video enables a pretrained video generation backbone to efficiently adapt to predict 3D scene flow. Co-trained on action-free human video and action-labeled robot teleoperation data, our model benefits from human-video pretraining and generalizes to unseen objects, motion primitives, and tasks. Replacing motion prediction with a video prediction objective degrades out-of-distribution generalization, while adding a video prediction target alongside motion lowers policy success rather than improving it. When motion, video, and action are predicted jointly, action tokens attend far more to motion than to video, suggesting that predicting video wastes model capacity. Together, these results indicate that motion effectively mediates the transfer from video pretraining to action. More information can be found at flomo-wam.github.io.

Abstract
Long-horizon reasoning remains a central challenge for large language models (LLMs) under sparse-reward regimes. We argue that this brittleness arises from two biases induced by complex reasoning spaces: an exploration bias, where models are drawn toward locally plausible but structurally unstable branches, and a compounding bias, where small local deviations accumulate across depth and suppress rare rewards. Abstract
We introduce ALBI (Agent for Learning By Induction), a neuro-symbolic ILP framework that replaces the ASP-based hypothesis generator in the Learning from Failures (LFF) paradigm with a Large Language Model (LLM), while retaining SWI-Prolog as a formal verifier. ALBI implements an iterative generate-test-constrain loop: the LLM proposes candidate hypotheses from structured prompts; Prolog tests each candidate; and diagnostic feedback drives refinement. We further introduce semantic anonymization, a metamorphic testing methodology, to distinguish structural symbolic induction from surface-level pattern matching on predicate names. Experiments on 30 standard ILP benchmarks show that ALBI achieves 100% success across three frontier LLMs (Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT 5.4 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6), matching Popper while requiring only 1.4-1.7 iterations on average. Under anonymization, all models maintain 100% success with a moderate iteration increase (1.36-1.67 times), providing evidence of robust symbolic reasoning.

Abstract

Abstract
Pervasive Capability Degradation (PCD) refers to the phenomenon in which supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while adapting a large language model (LLM) to a target task, often erodes the general capabilities acquired during pre-training. Prevailing explanations attribute PCD to macroscopic distribution shift and therefore lack microscopic manipulability, leaving existing mitigation methods without a complete theoretical foundation. In this paper, we first develop a bottom-up geometric theory of PCD grounded in feature interference under superposition: narrow-domain SFT data disrupt the almost-orthogonal feature structure formed during pre-training, and the resulting interference drives activations across decision hyperplanes in the output Laguerre-Voronoi geometry, flipping predictions on general tasks. This theory unifies existing methods into six paradigms and reveals both the source of their efficacy and their theoretical limits. We then prove that cross-entropy under one-hot supervision cannot resolve PCD on its own, and accordingly propose the AEGIS loss, which directly constrains the drift of activation margins along all competing token directions, thereby decoupling interference suppression from task adaptation. Across 3 model families and 14 SFT tasks, AEGIS outperforms representative methods of every paradigm on target tasks and pervasive capabilities simultaneously, at only about 1.3 times the computational cost of standard SFT. Its generality, scalability, and mechanistic interpretability offer a new perspective on fully resolving PCD.

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can now process hour-long videos, making extended multi-turn dialogue over a single video a natural mode of use. Sustaining such a dialogue requires more than answering each question in isolation: the model must retrieve and reuse information established earlier in the conversation, both what it has seen and what it has said, an ability we term in-context video memory. Existing video QA benchmarks leave this ability unmeasured: their questions are mutually independent, never reference one another, and each is answerable from the video alone, so nothing ever requires carrying information across turns. We introduce VideoMemBench, in which each sample pairs a single video with a long multi-turn dialogue, whose turns are connected by typed cross-turn dependencies: an Episodic Dependency refers back to a visual episode described in an earlier turn, probing memory of what the model has seen, while an Answer Dependency builds on the model's own earlier answer, probing memory of what the model has said. Every dependency is verified considering two properties: 1) necessity, meaning the question cannot be answered without its earlier turns, and 2) non-leakage, meaning the question does not restate the information from its earlier turns. To enforce these properties, we design a construction pipeline with three LLM roles: a planner, a generator, and a judger, with each dialogue reviewed by human experts. VideoMemBench comprises 326 videos with 2,476 turns, of which 1,387 depend on earlier turns, including multi-parent dependencies and long-range call-back. Among over 20 evaluated open- and closed-source MLLMs, Claude Opus 5 performs the best with an overall score of only 45.7%, highlighting the challenge of in-context video memory. Our dataset and evaluation are released.

Existing video QA benchmarks leave in-context video memory unmeasured because their questions are mutually independent and answerable from the video alone, so nothing requires carrying information across turns. The authors introduce VideoMemBench, in which each sample pairs one video with a long multi-turn dialogue whose turns are linked by typed cross-turn dependencies: Episodic Dependency refers back to a visual episode described earlier, probing memory of what the model has seen, while Answer Dependency builds on the model's own earlier answer, probing memory of what it has said. Abstract
Real-world geospatial decision support requires more than single-step retrieval or code generation. Disaster-response questions often involve multi-source workflows that combine spatial databases, map-derived features, satellite imagery, raster products, and reusable geospatial artifacts, where correctness depends on preserving spatial scope, temporal context, and intermediate execution state. While large language models and coding agents offer a promising interface for such workflows, they struggle with geospatial-specific failure modes, including handling large artifacts, selecting the wrong evidence source, losing spatial or temporal metadata, and applying invalid geometric operations. We introduce GeoFaham, a domain-specialized multi-agent system for geospatial disaster analytics. GeoFaham decomposes workflows into specialist agents for vector operations, map feature retrieval, imagery discovery, and raster analysis, coordinated by an Orchestrator with a dual-ledger control loop for dependency-aware planning, progress tracking, validation, and replanning. To support artifact-centric collaboration, GeoFaham uses a Response Store that persists full geospatial outputs and typed metadata outside the LLM context window while agents exchange compact identifiers and summaries. We also introduce GeoFaham-Bench, a 40-task benchmark grounded in 17 real-world disaster records, designed to evaluate multi-step geospatial reasoning, cross-source integration, raster-vector computation, artifact generation, and recovery under non-rigid ground truth. We evaluate GeoFaham on GeoFaham-Bench against strong general-purpose coding-agent baselines, including Codex, Claude Code, and OpenCode. GeoFaham achieves the best overall performance, scoring 0.940 versus the next-best 0.879, while using 7.5 times fewer tokens. Our code and benchmark are publicly available at https://github.com/microsoft/GeoFaham.

Authors: Hong Je-Gal, Jinwoo Kim, Chanbin Yi, Hyun-Suk Lee

First: 2026-11-03T14:29:36+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T14:29:36+00:00

Abstract
We investigate the automated generation of executable robot reinforcement learning (RL) policies from natural-language task descriptions. Rather than using large language models (LLMs) as execution-time decision makers, we use them as designers that construct RL pipelines before deployment, including Markov decision process (MDP) formulation and simulator implementation. We identify a phase-wise structure in this problem: MDP formulation is dependency-structured and sequential, whereas simulator implementation becomes modular once the formulation is fixed. Based on this structure, we propose ARPG, a dependency-aware agentic framework that performs sequential MDP modeling, localized verification and correction, and simulator-structured implementation while externalizing intermediate artifacts. ARPG targets a key failure mode of automated policy generation: executable code may still yield non-learnable policies when state, action, reward, and termination artifacts are not semantically aligned. Experiments in Isaac Sim show that ARPG improves modeling correctness, environment executability, and policy-generation success compared with monolithic LLM baselines and off-the-shelf agentic systems. Additional demonstrations on custom robotics, standard RL, and engineering-domain MDPs further suggest that the same modeling pipeline can be reused beyond the main benchmark suite.
