Micro-Edits: Computational Support for Fine-Scale Edits in Film
Authors: Nhan Tran, Hijung Valentina Shin, Abe Davis, Mackenzie Leake
First: 2026-10-29T17:59:49+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T17:59:49+00:00
Comments: UIST 2026 Best Paper Honorable Mention. Project page: https://microedits.github.io/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
In film editing, every frame matters. While macro, content-level edits shape the high-level narrative of a film, small local edits often control timing and direct visual attention. For example, editors can adjust the precise timing of clips to control the length of a meaningful silence, or cut to shots that highlight a specific character's reactions to important moments. These small, detailed edits are slow and tedious to make in standard editing tools because users have to map high-level narrative goals to low-level frame-by-frame adjustments. At the same time, these fine-scale adjustments, which we call micro-edits, can be nuanced and expressive, with different choices serving different creative goals. In this work, we start by conducting a formative study with professional editors to understand the structure of micro-edits and how they are used. We then derive a mathematical formalization for this structure to develop tools that leverage AI and optimization to make exploring micro-edits simpler and more efficient for users. Finally, we validate our tools in multiple user studies and a wide range of video examples.

world2vec: Mechanistic world models through latent dynamics learning
Authors: Tobias Schmidt, Rodrigo Gonzalez Laiz, Krisztián Szuppinger, Steffen Schneider

Abstract
World models can achieve strong control performance without faithfully capturing the dynamics of their environment, limiting their potential for mechanistic interpretability and scientific discovery. Here, we introduce world2vec to learn mechanistic world models without reward information directly from observations and actions. World2vec learns latent dynamics that faithfully reproduce the true system over long prediction horizons, with at least one order of magnitude fewer parameters than DreamerV3, TD-MPC2 and LeWM. This enables state of the art performance in controlling continuous environments from pixels and accurate system identification across a benchmark of 71 attractors, demonstrating how world models are amenable to scientific discovery.

Fine-tuning Multi-modal LLMs with ART: Art-based Reinforcement Training
Authors: Michal Chudoba, Sergey Alyaev, Petra Galuscakova, Tomasz Wiktorski
First: 2026-06-10T09:30:37+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T17:56:43+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Adapting Large Language Models (LLMs) to specific tasks usually involves fine-tuning: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) edits the weights, while soft prompting prepends trainable virtual tokens. Both change how an already-optimized model is served, so serving many task-specific adapters at high throughput requires adapter-aware infrastructure. We propose Art-Based Reinforcement Training (ART), which optimizes a single auxiliary input image to specialize a frozen Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) for a specific task. At inference time, ART simply includes this image in each standard multimodal prompt, requiring no changes to the serving engine. We compare ART with LoRA and soft prompting across five reasoning benchmarks for four small MLLMs from three architectures. While different methods perform best for different tasks, ART ranks first or second in 10 of 20 benchmark-MLLM pairs, with its best results on the two math word problem benchmarks (GSM8k and SVAMP). It is significantly ahead of the next best adapter in 2 cases, within the noise of the next best adapter in 6, and significantly above the text-only baseline in 18. ART therefore reaches accuracy close to LoRA and soft prompts while serving faster than LoRA. In the vLLM serving stack, ART reduces throughput by 6% to 14% relative to text-only requests, compared to LoRA's 13% to 93% slowdown. Beyond efficient serving, ART artifacts are plain images, making them easy to store, distribute, and share. This approach keep task-specific adaptation outside the model weights, so the same frozen model can support different tasks without separate model variants.

L3G-World: Real-Time Infinite World Generation with Latent 3D Gaussian Memory
Authors: Jinhyeok Choi, JoungBin Lee, Jongmin Lee, Minkyeong Jeon, Jiyoung Kim, Kaeun Han, Yoonjin Yoon, Seungryong Kim
First: 2026-10-29T17:54:42+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T17:54:42+00:00
Comments: Project page: https://cvlab-kaist.github.io/L3G-World/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Interactive world models generate visual content conditioned on user actions, yet maintaining spatial consistency over long-horizon exploration in real time remains challenging. Existing approaches rely on either temporal context or explicit 3D memory, both of which scale poorly because memory grows with the length of the generated stream. Moreover, explicit 3D memories condition latent video generators by re-encoding RGB renderings, which precludes real-time generation. In this paper, we introduce a Latent 3D Gaussian Memory (L3G) that combines the efficiency of feed-forward reconstruction with a compact, rapidly renderable scene representation. Building on this memory, we propose L3G-World, in which a memory updater and a video diffusion transformer (DiT) alternate autoregressively to generate spatially consistent worlds. The memory updater spawns Gaussians in newly observed regions and refines existing ones, so that the memory grows with scene extent rather than stream length. Each Gaussian further stores a VAE latent feature, allowing the memory to be rendered directly into the DiT's latent space without VAE re-encoding. Experiments show that L3G-World achieves state-of-the-art generation quality and long-horizon spatial consistency, with up to $38.6\times$ fewer 3D primitives, $3.2\times$ faster memory updates, and $359\times$ faster memory rendering relative to the most efficient prior 3D-memory baseline for each metric, enabling real-time world generation at 9 FPS.

Abstract
We present TextSeal2, a distortion-free watermark for large language models that keeps its signal under speculative decoding: tokens that replace rejected drafts remain watermarked with the same keys, and the acceptance rate is unchanged. It also preserves output diversity and adds no measurable inference latency. Its predecessor, TextSeal, restores diversity in the Gumbel-max scheme by routing each token to one of two keys via a coin flip. This is suboptimal because the detector averages the scores from both keys without knowing which key was used. TextSeal2 instead merges two or more keys into a single virtual key and couples it to fresh noise through AutoMark's Gaussian copula. Any number of keys, including publicly shared keys, can be merged this way without diluting detection. Under speculative decoding, TextSeal2 verifies keyed drafts with one more exponential race, run on the leftovers of the draft races. By memorylessness, this race reproduces block verification, so the output follows the target model exactly. We evaluate TextSeal2 on three LLMs. At equal output token diversity, TextSeal2 detects 8 to 22 percentage points more 100-token answers than TextSeal at a false-positive rate of $10^{-3}$, and is comparable to textGrain while requiring no post-logits optimization. Under speculative decoding, it detects 18 and 22 percentage points more 200-token answers than TextSeal and textGrain, respectively, at the same acceptance rate.

Fight World Model: Customizable Combat through Visual Interaction Proxies
Authors: Jiahua Li, Zixu John, Tom Zhong, Fuping Wu, Tianhao Xu, Ruicheng Zhang, Jun Zhou, Zunnan Xu
First: 2026-10-29T17:53:02+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T17:53:02+00:00

Abstract
A playable, programmable game world must maintain persistent state, enforce executable rules, and support extensible gameplay mechanics. Existing video world models can generate plausible combat, yet struggle to keep hit reactions and health updates consistent with game rules. We introduce Fight World Model (FWM), which separates rule-governed combat execution from video rendering. An executable engine maintains state and resolves interactions. A deterministic compiler encodes these outcomes and attempted actions in a spatially and temporally aligned interaction proxy. The proxy aligns action and skill windows, brief hit reactions, and persistent health by fighter identity and timing. These cues can be controlled separately to render interactions under modified rules. Identity-bound references preserve character appearance across side switches. We train FWM on FightWorld Corpus, spanning 52 skill effects, 187 character-appearance variants, and 15 arenas. Without retraining the renderer, FWM supports damage scaling, limited-hit immunity, lifesteal, and multi-stage skill mechanisms beyond the native game. It also allows character and arena customization. Experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of FWM, which outperforms existing game world models in action fidelity, hit reactions, health-state consistency, and character appearance preservation. Our project is available at https://fight-world-model.github.io.

Abstract
An autonomous research laboratory could conduct an entire study, from formulating questions to preparing manuscripts. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers one route towards this goal. Recent AI systems take on several research roles, but may draw conclusions that the evidence does not support. Here, we present an autonomous research laboratory in which agents based on large language models assess one another's work and can stop or redirect investigations. They can generate and reject research questions, require separate theoretical checks and conduct experiments designed to challenge their claims. We demonstrate these capabilities by investigating ensembles of quantum spins in diamond. The agents derived the minimum number of calibration settings needed to predict responses to a defined family of microwave pulse sequences. They checked the result computationally and designed experiments to distinguish competing predictions. An initial experiment agreed with the theoretical predictions, but repeated reference measurements showed that the signal was drifting during data collection. The agents therefore treated the result as inconclusive and revised the measurement protocol without relaxing the acceptance criteria. The revised experiment met those criteria and supported the theoretical predictions. This study demonstrates that collaborating AI agents can select research questions, derive theoretical results and test their predictions experimentally.

Abstract
Current zero-shot anomaly detection methods often rely on complex prompt-learning techniques or computationally expensive sliding-window strategies to achieve fine-grained visual alignment to detect small-scale anomalies. This reliance stems from the fundamental limitation of standard CLIP architectures, where [CLS] token attention and spatial patch pooling struggle to capture subtle, localized anomaly features. While prompt-learning approaches aim to bridge this gap, they typically require a large number of ground-truth segmentation masks from auxiliary datasets, which are often unavailable in specialized domains. In this work, we show an emerging property of multi-head attention pooling in vision-language models: remarkable zero-shot anomaly localization and detection right out of the box. We translate this property into a single-pass anomaly detection technique, RADIOlogy, that does not require dense, windowed execution and significantly outperforms other training-free baselines. Instead, the method relies on a simple contrastive scoring approach that compares the average similarity between vision features and a suite of normal and abnormal prompts. Furthermore, we leverage the agglomerative features of RADIO models to enhance pixel-level anomaly segmentation using a dense anomaly refinement pipeline. Finally, we show how the vision-language family of training-free zero-shot anomaly detection techniques can be applied successfully to the medical domain with minimal modifications. Abstract
Building and scaling computer-using agents (CUAs) across desktop, mobile, and web environments requires reliable task-completion verification for data curation, evaluation, and reinforcement learning. Typical automated approaches prompt vision-language models (VLMs) to judge trajectories or generate programs to inspect application state. However, both rely on partial views of complex digital environments. VLM judges are restricted to supplied observations, while generated programs depend on accessible state. These views can omit decisive evidence or leave completion judgments unsupported. Tailoring separate verifiers to different platforms and access settings adds further engineering cost. We present VeriCUA, a platform-agnostic framework for verification through agentic exploration. It treats verification as dynamic evidence gathering and assessment within a shared procedure for online and offline settings across platforms and observation modalities. After inspecting the available context to construct a task rubric, the verifier selects GUI or code checks for unresolved requirements and combines their findings under explicit evidence rules. Experiments show improved agreement with reference labels over the compared baselines across these settings, with the largest offline gains on hard cases and stronger rejection of false completion claims online. Further analyses connect these gains to complementary evidence and test-time exploration.

Abstract
Realistic, fast robotics simulations could enable large-scale training of embodied AI models that transfer to the real world. However, despite decades of progress, available simulations cover only a narrow range of real-world tasks and physical phenomena. The core bottleneck is that creating better simulations requires significant expert effort and therefore does not scale. To address this, we introduce Gym-Anything for Robotics, a framework in which coding agents create realistic robotics simulations. Our core insight is that simulation creation is a coding agent task, so improvements in coding agents translate directly into richer and more faithful simulations. We select simulations based on U.S. GDP data to cover economically valuable physical work, define what makes a good simulation and a good task, and develop an adversarial creation-audit loop that checks each simulation and task against these definitions. Using this pipeline, we build RoboWorld, a set of 25 simulations with 25 test and 175 training RL environments of varying difficulty. Even the best frontier models fail on every RoboWorld test task, achieving an average reward of only 0.06. Building on RoboWorld, we introduce several new ways for frontier models to improve, including in-context learning across repeated attempts, a privileged teacher that reads the simulator state and teaches a student in context, self-assisted learning from the model's own privileged experience, and code policies that generate demonstrations across varied scenes to train vision-language-action models (VLAs) such as $π_{0.5}$. Each of these substantially improves performance. We release our pipeline, RL environments, and benchmark to facilitate future research. Abstract
Predicting how many tokens a large language model (LLM) will decode, using only prefill-phase hidden states, is a key primitive for inference scheduling. We show that current benchmarks conflate predictor quality with task hardness: when the target model, dataset, or sampling configuration changes, reported accuracy collapses even though the predictor is unchanged. We trace this instability to an overlooked error source---the inherent stochasticity of autoregressive decoding---and derive bounds that every prefill-side method must obey. Our analysis treats decoding as a stopped stochastic process. The per-step stopping probability of a response is not constant but ramps up over hundreds of steps, so the decode length inherits a central limit theorem from the many local decisions that determine where a response ends; this yields per-prompt normality with an explicit error term, together with an affine variance law $σ=αμ+b$ whose slope is the coefficient of variation of the number of response segments. Empirically, the normal law reproduces the per-prompt MAE and the central mass of the decode-length distribution to within a few percent, and the residual departures from normality (mild right skew, slight excess kurtosis) are the ones predicted by the sampling-noise correction. Under fixed model, data, and sampling conditions, a broad design space of predictors (MLP, EGTP, FiLM, soft-label, and Mixture-of-Experts variants) converges to similar performance close to the bound, confirming that \emph{system error dominates model error}. We argue for a benchmarking protocol that fixes the model, data, and sampling parameters and that reports relative-error metrics alongside absolute ones. Abstract
Images reveal appearances, but counting often asks about physical objects. A single entity may be visible directly, in several reflections, or through glass, so detecting every visible occurrence does not determine how many entities are present. Existing visual counting benchmarks rarely separate these two operations. We introduce PhysCount, a benchmark for evaluating whether multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can distinguish what appears in an image from what physically exists in the scene. PhysCount contains 546 real-world images and 4,595 human-written questions organized into 651 target-specific groups. These results expose a gap between recognizing what is visible and correctly recovering the physical entities behind those observations.

Abstract
Safety alignment for Large Language Models is heavily focused on English with limited multilingual representation, particularly damaging low-resource language performance. Related open training datasets are often limited in size or do not follow region-localized harmful topics to broaden the scope of alignment beyond cultures. We propose a scalable template-based augmentation pipeline to produce massive multilingual refusal and benign corpora, extracting safe templates from harmful prompts, translating them and then filling in the slots with culturally authentic entities, both harmful and benign. As a result, we release MSafeTemp, a validated corpus spanning 60 languages useful for multilingual safety alignment and evaluation. Using the dataset, we show that English-only evaluation misjudges multilingual safety: for several models, attacks that fail in English succeed once translated, while culturally localized variants expose further failures that translation alone misses. We also confirm that augmenting safety training data through templates allows for higher diversity and quality of alignment, resulting in improved safety performance beyond English. MSafeTemp and the respective pipeline are aimed at facilitating research and development of safer and more responsible models for less-represented cultures.

Abstract
AI has made remarkable progress in mathematics, solving challenging problems with machine-checkable proofs in Lean, yet typically relying on predefined mathematical definitions and formal specifications. This raises a natural question: can it reason rigorously about an unknown world? Abstract
We present LucidLyra, a framework that makes generatively reconstructed 3D scenes directly queryable for semantics and instances in a feed-forward manner from video latents. While generative 3D reconstruction can synthesize virtual environments from a single image, downstream applications in robotics and physical AI require not only visual realism but also access to scene semantics and object identities. Existing 3D understanding approaches, however, require captured multi-view scenes, have limited generalization capabilities, or rely on expensive per-scene processing and optimization. To bridge this gap, our method distills complementary knowledge from a camera-controlled video diffusion model and frozen 2D vision foundation models into a unified model to jointly infer 3D Gaussians encoding geometry, appearance, open-vocabulary semantics, and class-agnostic instance cues. The resulting scenes support novel-view rendering, open-vocabulary segmentation, and object grouping without per-scene optimization, while training requires neither manual 3D annotations nor captured multi-view data. Our method outperforms baselines combining state-of-the-art 3DGS segmentation approaches with generative 3D scene reconstruction on curated synthetic and real-image-conditioned benchmarks, while preserving reconstruction quality. Furthermore, our latent formulation naturally extends to monocular video, reconstructing structured dynamic 3D scenes across time. By turning renderable but unstructured outputs into queryable 3D worlds, LucidLyra enables downstream applications including 3D scene editing, 3D asset mining, and robotic simulation.

Abstract
We introduce Continuous-Action World Models (CoAWM), a framework for learning cross-embodiment spatiotemporal dynamics priors that support robot action generation across embodiments and execution frequencies. At its core is a shared latent function space that captures the spatial and temporal structure of robot actions. We learn this space through two stages. First, function pretraining brings trajectories from different embodiments and sampling frequencies into a common representation, learning shared dynamics priors through action reconstruction and consistency across temporal sampling views. By accommodating heterogeneous robot trajectories, this stage broadens the range of data sources available for pretraining and facilitates scaling to larger and more diverse datasets. Second, we connect the pretrained function space to a world-model backbone, enabling observations, language instructions, and the current robot state to condition the prediction of a latent action function. A single decoder takes any query time and returns the corresponding end-effector action. By separating shared dynamics priors from task-conditioned action selection and temporal sampling, our approach provides a unified foundation for transferring robot experience across embodiments and control frequencies.

Abstract

Recent advances have further incorporated future prediction to enhance the understanding of environmental dynamics and task progression. However, existing methods often model short-term dynamics and long-term task objectives separately, while directly incorporating predicted futures into action policies without sufficiently considering their relevance to current action requirements. Furthermore, Action-Guided Factorized World Retrieval adaptively retrieves relevant future information from the structured latent world based on evolving action representations during denoising, providing targeted guidance for action generation. Extensive experiments on two simulation benchmarks and six real-world robotic manipulation tasks demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach.

Abstract

Abstract
Graphical User Interfaces (GUIs) serve as the dominant medium for human-computer interaction, yet building GUI agents that generalize across the vast diversity of real-world interface environments, with the same flexibility and robustness that humans naturally exhibit, remains unsolved. Notably, GUI data are inherently non-stationary: the continual emergence of previously unseen interface instances (e.g., novel domains and resolutions) induces persistent distribution shifts, significantly impeding the continual learning of existing GUI agents. Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) has attracted considerable attention as a promising approach. Nevertheless, RFT exhibits pronounced instability in its grounding capability, where rollout outcomes are highly imbalanced and fixed clipping can suppress valuable exploration. More importantly, policy probability alone does not reveal the semantic role of a grounding prediction. To address these challenges, we propose GUI-AC, a continual RFT framework that jointly characterizes grounding rollouts through advantage and policy probability. This yields a four-region view of grounding behavior and enables targeted optimization of Exploration and Overconfidence. GUI-AC further introduces grounding certainty to support two core mechanisms: (i) Adaptive Advantage, which strengthens the negative correction for overconfident grounding failures; and (ii) Dynamic Clipping, which relaxes the upper clipping bound for exploratory grounding rollouts. Abstract
Low-bit quantization enables efficient deployment of large language models by reducing memory footprint and computational cost. Achieving high accuracy at low precision requires selecting quantization ranges, linear transformations, and weight rounding decisions. Existing approaches typically optimize these components independently using local reconstruction objectives, which fail to account for the varying impact of quantization errors on downstream predictions. Direct optimization of task-level metrics would provide a more faithful objective, but is challenging due to the discontinuous nature of quantization operations and the high computational cost of evaluating their downstream effects. In this work, we derive a Hessian-guided approximation of the output change induced by joint weight and activation quantization. Based on a tractable Fisher approximation, we formulate a unified post-training quantization framework that enables the joint optimization of quantization ranges, linear transformations, and weight rounding under a common output-sensitivity objective. The resulting method remains practical for modern large language models and consistently outperforms state-of-the-art transformation and rounding methods across both channel-wise and block-wise quantization settings.

Abstract
Radiological risk stratification and management planning are essential downstream tasks in medical imaging. Automating these tasks is challenging because clinical guideline criteria are distributed across prose, tables, and cross-referenced definitions. We present Guideline-derived Decision Graph (GDGraph), a framework that decouples guideline compilation from case evaluation. During compilation, a large language model partitions guidelines into section-based chunks, extracts local decision subgraphs with linked definitions, and aggregates related branches into an executable decision graph. Across 347 cases spanning renal cyst, adnexal mass, and thyroid nodule guidelines, GDGraph improves System One accuracy from 57.3% to 90.8%, outperforming retrieval-augmented GPT-4o (80.1%) and graph-guided GPT-4o with chain-of-thought (82.7%) at substantially lower latency and cost. Abstract
Large language model serving is limited by facility power. Energy per token determines how many tokens a datacenter can serve, while a latency target bounds how quickly each user receives them. During decode, every weight is read from memory for every generated token. Batching amortizes this read across users but makes no individual user faster. Speculative decoding does: a drafter proposes several future tokens, and the target model verifies them in a single forward pass that reuses the same weight read. Because every drafted token must be verified whether or not it is accepted, the energy speculation saves depends on how many drafted tokens are accepted. This saving is hard to pin down. It varies across target models from different attention families and across drafting schemes, and it depends on the text being served. Every drafter we evaluate accepts fewer tokens on conversation than on code, and every trained drafter accepts fewer on Romansh, a low-resource language, than on English. We measure speculative decoding under production serving conditions (SGLang, CUDA graphs enabled) on H100 and H200 GPUs, at batch sizes up to 64 across 5 corpora. On dense models, verification, not drafting, dominates the energy cost. The saving shrinks as the batch grows, because batching has already shared the weight read that speculation would divide among the accepted tokens. At large batch sizes, block drafters push verification past the balance point between arithmetic and memory traffic into the compute-bound regime, where the saving becomes a loss, and a drafter with low acceptance raises energy per token. Meeting a tighter latency target with speculation then costs additional energy. Larger batches lower energy per token without speculation. Speculation adds to that saving only when acceptance is high, and on the model we tested it leaves little headroom for latency-preserving DVFS.

Abstract
Building general game agents requires both high-level task reasoning and low-latency action control in rapidly changing visual environments. These capabilities favor models with substantially different computational profiles, making them difficult to realize within a single model. In this work, we present TaskFrame, a hierarchical planner-controller framework that connects task-level planning with frame-level keyboard-and-mouse control. A vision-language task planner decomposes a high-level goal into an ordered sequence of atomic instructions and is optimized through supervised learning and dependency-aware GRPO. The hidden states of special goal tokens that terminate each atomic instruction provide continuous goal embeddings that are cached and reused across control steps. A causal Transformer controller combines the embedding of the active goal with recent observations to predict discrete keyboard and mouse-button actions and continuous camera motion at every game step. We train the system in three stages: planner training, controller pretraining, and joint alignment. Experiments in Minecraft show that TaskFrame is competitive with general-purpose vision-language models on instruction decomposition, and that in closed-loop evaluation the agent achieves a higher combat success rate than the evaluated baselines.

Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) power platforms like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot, enabling rich interactions across text, images, and videos. Unlike text-only workloads, multimodal requests are inherently heterogeneous, differing by orders of magnitude in latency and memory footprint. Existing LLM serving systems, designed for homogeneous text workloads, struggle under this heterogeneity: large image and video requests monopolize GPU resources, causing severe head-of-line blocking and degrading the responsiveness of lightweight, latency-critical text queries. Then, it lets sand flow quickly through pebbles and rocks, maximizing GPU utilization while ensuring fast responsiveness for lightweight requests and controlled execution for heavier ones. To achieve this, RPS-Serve estimates the GPU admission cost of each request, classifies requests into rocks, pebbles, and sand using lightweight clustering, and applies priority-based scheduling with aging to balance performance across modalities. Evaluation across state-of-the-art MLLMs shows that RPS-Serve increases GPU utilization by 65% and reduces TTFT by up to 9.3x, on average across models, compared to disaggregation-based multi-modal systems.

Abstract
Large AI models increasingly rely on distributed GPU execution, yet it remains unclear whether established anomaly detection methods transfer effectively to this setting. We introduce GF-Bench, a PyTorch-based framework for generating controlled failures in distributed GPU workloads, and a dataset produced with it. The framework supports extensible workload and fault-injection logic; based on this design, the current release targets failures visible in multi-GPU communication logs and provides 220 labeled two-GPU runs covering normal behavior, CUDA out-of-memory fail-stop faults, and persistent-straggler fail-slow faults. Using GF-Bench, we evaluate three sequence-based methods (DeepLog, LogAnomaly, and LogBERT) and the large language model (LLM)-based LogPrompt with in-context learning (ICL) and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting strategies on parsed logs, and additionally evaluate LogPrompt on raw logs. GF-Bench provides a reproducible basis for studying cost-efficient anomaly detection methods and can be extended to additional GPU workloads and failure types.

Authors: Lang Chen, Zongxin Xu, Borui Wang, Anran Liu, Shitong Chen

Abstract
We revisit advantage estimation in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and observe that any token-level advantage admits a two-stage factorization: a \emph{sample-level baselining} step that converts scalar verifier rewards into a comparable sequence-level signal, and a \emph{token-level credit assignment} step that distributes that signal over the trajectory. Existing estimators typically address only one of these stages: affine baselines preserve the shape of each task's reward law, so heavy tails and binary cliffs propagate into the gradient, while token-level credit rules tie the size of each token's update to raw confidence scales and need not conserve the total credit issued by the verifier. We propose \textbf{TACE} (Two-stage Advantage and Credit Estimation), a two-stage estimator that addresses both stages. At stage~(S1), we replace linear standardization with a one-dimensional optimal-transport map to a standard normal, which gives a common population reward law across tasks and caps finite-sample outliers deterministically. At stage~(S2), we rank-calibrate a stop-gradient evidence contrast within each trajectory and renormalize the clipped token weights to have mean one, so credit is redistributed across tokens while its total mass and sign are conserved. We prove population-level inter-task moment equity, a deterministic tail bound, and mass-conservation, boundedness, and sign-anchoring properties that affine baselines and unnormalized token weights do not satisfy. On a multimodal reasoning suite, TACE obtains the strongest results on a majority of the benchmarks but does not dominate every setting.

Abstract
Computer-use agents built on large language models (LLMs) work in software that can change while they act: an appointment moves, a policy is revised, a record is updated. Testing agents under such changes is harder than it looks. When an agent fails, the cause may be the agent, but it may also be a change that did not fire as intended, an update the agent never received, or a grader that scored the episode wrongly. We introduce EnvOS, a framework for building and certifying environments that change mid-task. Before any agent runs, EnvOS checks that each change fires correctly, that the task remains solvable, and that the grader separates correct from incorrect behavior; after each run, separate code re-checks the verdict from archived evidence. We apply it to stale information, where a value the agent has already seen changes before the agent acts on it. In clinic booking, Claude Opus 5 succeeds on 0/16 tasks when nothing extra is shown, 2/16 when reminded to re-check the source, and 15/16 when the updated value is shown; the gain over the reminder holds after correcting for all tests (p = 0.00293). In insurance-claim approval, Opus 5 re-checks on its own and succeeds on 10/10, whereas Claude Sonnet 5 rises from 1/10 to 9/10 when the current policy is shown again, a gain significant in its pre-registered test but not after correcting for all tests. Whether an agent caught a change thus varied with the model and the interface. We also stress-test the grading itself: on corrupted episodes designed blind from the rules, the EnvOS pipeline accepted none of 26 as clean, and on an externally generated set it caught 25 of 39, against 4 for simple log checks. Because every EnvOS environment is generated from a seed and graded by an audited automatic verifier, the same environments are natural candidates for training agents with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards.
