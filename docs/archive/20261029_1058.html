Can LLMs Uncover Hidden Physical Laws through Free Exploration?
Authors: Zhen Yang, Wen Wang, Xander Xu, Ying-Cong Chen
First: 2026-10-27T17:57:00+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-27T17:57:00+00:00
Comments: https://zhenyangcs.github.io/LongChang-website/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Scientific discovery often begins with an unexpected observation that prompts a new research question. In a world with altered physical laws, can large language models (LLMs) notice unexpected behavior, such as heavier objects falling more slowly, and discover the law through experiments without being told what to investigate? To evaluate this ability, we introduce LongChang, an automated framework that creates simulated worlds with altered physics, where one or more laws differ from ordinary physics. The evaluated LLMs act as explorers, manipulating objects and building tools to investigate their own questions without research tasks or hints about these laws. Separate LLM agents generate and review scenes, review tool designs, and evaluate reports for correctness and experimental support using the altered physical laws and exploration records. These records guide scene evolution for further exploration, allowing the full cycle to run without human intervention. Among 12 LLMs, GPT-6-Astra achieves a discovery rate of 16.7%, while the other 11 score 0%. The exploration records reveal uninvestigated anomalies and only partial identification of the altered physical laws.

Scaling Laws for Tool-Use Agents via Controllable Environment Synthesis
Authors: Andrew Szot, Samira Abnar, Omar Attia, Abbas Kazerouni, Michael Kirchhof, Cheng-Yu Hsieh, Raviteja Vemulapalli, Alexander Toshev
First: 2026-10-27T17:56:46+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-27T17:56:46+00:00

Abstract
We explore how to build performant large language model (LLM) tool-use agents that complete complex, multi-step tasks. Specifically, we study how data composition in synthetically generated tool-use environments affects the training of such agents. To do so, we propose the SynthAPI pipeline, which explicitly controls the diversity of generated environments along the axes of tools, tasks, and the databases on which the tools operate. By systematically varying SynthAPI's generation controls in 458,752 tasks across 849 trained models of four different sizes, we derive a scaling law that predicts downstream tool-use agent generalization based on the scale and composition of the synthesized environments. These predictable relationships reveal several lessons for scaling tool-use agent data. As data budgets grow, scaling tool and database diversity becomes more effective than increasing the number of tasks, and increasing data complexity yields larger gains. Scaling according to these findings yields agents that outperform models trained on data from prior work in large-scale tool-use agent and synthetic environment generation across four evaluation benchmarks.

Corrupted but Correct: Why Vision-Language Models Lie to Themselves Internally
Authors: Arun Josephraj Arokiaraj, Chenyi Huang, Zekun Wu, Adriano Koshiyama

Comments: Accepted at the VLM4RWD Workshop (Grounded and Faithful Vision-Language Models for Real-World Deployment), NeurIPS 2026. 8 pages, 2 figures, 3 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
A targeted adversarial perturbation can drive a vision-language model's (VLM's) teacher-forced training loss for a fixed target caption to near zero, yet the same model, allowed to generate freely, produces the original, correct description with no trace of the target. We call this dissociation the train/inference gap, and give it a precise mechanistic account on Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct using a controlled two-stage PGD attack on 200 held-out COCO images. First, we show that image-level pixel statistics, including a correctly re-implemented, texture-based attackability measure from the CNN robustness literature, have essentially no predictive power over which images are corrupted (best predictor r=-0.050, p=0.484; ridge regression R^2=0.069). Second, using the logit lens, we localise the gap to a single autoregressive step: the rank of the target token, conditioned on the correct first token already being generated, is fixed at exactly 3,488 out of 152,064 vocabulary entries for every image and every condition, with zero variance. Third, tracking target-token rank across all 28 LLM decoder layers reveals that the visual encoder corrupts every image's representation by a comparable margin regardless of eventual outcome, but the language model decoder then differentially arbitrates: amplifying the corrupted signal for susceptible images and actively suppressing it, past its clean-image baseline, for resistant ones (p<0.001, rank-biserial r=0.579). A linear probe on the merger hidden state separates these two outcomes with AUC=0.858, though we flag a circularity concern in this estimate. Together these results argue that adversarial robustness in autoregressive VLMs is substantially a property of the language decoder's prior, not the visual encoder, with direct implications for where faithfulness evaluations and defenses for deployed VLM systems should be targeted.

Perceiving and Predicting Dynamic Sports: Spatiotemporal Intelligence in Vision Models for Egocentric Tennis Video
Authors: Tudor Braicu, Joseph DelPreto, Jared Boyer, Mónika Farsang, Maximilian Stölzle, Tim Niklas Seyde, Daniela Rus
First: 2026-10-27T17:47:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-27T17:47:14+00:00
Comments: 8 pages, 5 figures. Project page: https://tennis-vision.csail.mit.edu/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Agile robots require spatiotemporal intelligence to interpret scenes, reason about interactions, and anticipate future events. Yet it remains unclear how well foundation vision models support these capabilities, particularly from a moving egocentric camera, which is a natural view for robots. To investigate this, we collect an annotated tennis dataset and train a suite of vision models on spatiotemporal tasks. Our dataset contains egocentric video synchronized with five allocentric videos, estimated global positions, labeled contact times, and annotated stroke types. We then evaluate seven prediction tasks from egocentric images: 2D ego player and 3D ball localization, future player and ball trajectories, forehand or backhand stroke anticipation, time to the next ego player ball contact, and 3D ball position at that contact. We compare image encoders, video encoders, and vision-language models with jointly trained prediction heads. The best performing models achieve mean current-frame localization errors of 0.14 m for the ego player and 0.54 m for the ball, future forecasting errors of 0.34 m for the ego player and 0.91 m for the ball over 1.1 s, and a mean contact-time prediction error of 0.14 s. Auxiliary depth information substantially improves 3D ball localization, suggesting that inferring metric depth remains a challenge. Longer image histories do not improve ball forecasting, indicating that representations exploit scene context rather than rely solely on inter-frame motion. Finally, we propose a conditional diffusion model to generate whole-body kinematic tennis motion from vision encoder predictions on egocentric video at real-time rates, adapting movements as predictions are refined. These results move towards a deeper understanding of how foundation vision models can support real-time dynamic robots using egocentric video.

Optimizing Spectral Signature in Code Backdoor Detection
Authors: Quoc Hung Le, Thanh Le-Cong, Bach Le, Bowen Xu

Comments: Accepted at the IEEE International Conference on Software Maintenance and Evolution (ICSME 2026). 12 pages, 4 figures

Abstract
As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into software development workflows, they also become prime targets for adversarial attacks. Among these, backdoor attacks are a significant threat, allowing attackers to manipulate model outputs through hidden triggers embedded in training data. Detecting such backdoors remains a challenge, and one promising approach is the use of Spectral Signature defense methods that identify poisoned data by analyzing feature representations through eigenvectors. While some prior works have explored Spectral Signatures for backdoor detection in neural networks, recent studies suggest that these methods may not be optimally effective for code models. In this paper, we revisit the applicability of Spectral Signature-based defenses in the context of backdoor attacks on code models. We systematically evaluate their effectiveness under various attack scenarios and defense configurations, analyzing their strengths and limitations. We found that the widely used setting of Spectral Signature in code backdoor detection is often suboptimal. Hence, we explored the impact of different settings of the key factors. We discovered a new proxy metric that can more accurately estimate the actual performance of Spectral Signature without model retraining after the defense. Our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the configurations of Spectral Signature defenses in securing code models and offer insights into potential improvements for future research.

VideoLVR: Latent Visual Reasoning over Grounded Video Evidence
Authors: Shilong Li, Xingyuan Bu, Hao Shi, Yun Xing, Weixin Feng, Qiangpeng Yang, Shilei Wen
First: 2026-10-27T17:41:56+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-27T17:41:56+00:00
Comments: 13 pages
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Chain-of-thought reasoning has substantially advanced video understanding in multimodal large language models (MLLMs). However, language-based reasoning traces do not provide a reliable mechanism for models to revisit relevant visual evidence, leading to visual forgetting and impairing their ability to reason over video content. Existing approaches maintain visual grounding during reasoning in two main ways: they either refer to evidence using textual coordinates, which specify symbolic locations rather than the visual content itself, or reinsert cropped regions or frames into the multimodal context, preserving visual details at the cost of substantial context expansion. To address these limitations, we introduce VideoLVR, which constructs latent visual evidence from local regions at relevant video moments, thereby providing visual support for intermediate reasoning steps. To supervise latent visual evidence generation, we introduce a visual token prediction objective that formulates visual token reconstruction as prediction over a vocabulary derived from the input video, thereby unifying visual evidence reconstruction and text generation within the same token prediction framework. This shared prediction framework enables MLLMs to reason over interleaved text tokens and latent visual states while integrating evidence from different moments. We further extend VideoLVR to reinforcement learning by treating sampled visual tokens as actions alongside text tokens and jointly optimizing the policy using final-answer rewards. By efficiently revisiting task-relevant visual information during reasoning, VideoLVR improves video understanding without substantially expanding the multimodal context. Experiments on multiple video benchmarks demonstrate its effectiveness, especially on tasks requiring fine-grained visual evidence.

Abstract
Large language model (LLM)-driven agents are emerging as a powerful new paradigm for solving complex problems. Despite the empirical success of these practices, a theoretical framework to understand and unify their macroscopic dynamics remains lacking. This paper proposes a method based on the least action principle to estimate the underlying generative directionality of LLMs embedded within agents. By experimentally measuring the transition probabilities between LLM-generated states, we report evidence consistent with a tendency toward detailed balance in LLM-generated transitions, indicating that LLM generation may not be achieved by generally learning rule sets and strategies, but rather by implicitly learning a class of underlying potential functions that may transcend different LLM architectures and prompt templates. To our knowledge, this provides an effective macroscopic description of LLM generative dynamics that does not depend on specific model details. This work is an attempt to establish a macroscopic dynamics theory of complex AI systems, aiming to elevate the study of AI agents from a collection of engineering practices to a science built on effective measurements that are predictable and quantifiable.

Abstract
World models are widely proposed as core components for enabling agents to reason over long temporal and spatial horizons. Yet current evaluations often emphasize task performance while providing limited insight into whether learned world models acquire predictive latent dynamics that reflect the underlying structure of the environment. Here we introduce the concept of faithful latent world models as an equivariance condition between the environment's transition map and the learned latent dynamics. We propose latent rollout consistency (LRC) as a metric to evaluate action-conditioned latent prediction. Strikingly, we find that across the studied state of the art latent world models, none solves the task by actually faithfully modeling the dynamics of the environment.

Abstract
Automatic soccer event spotting supports highlight generation, video indexing, and sports analytics, but existing methods depend on large annotated datasets and soccer-specific training. We present a zero-shot framework that detects, classifies, and temporally localizes events in full-length soccer broadcasts without task-specific parameter training. Each match is segmented into overlapping clips represented by a compact visual storyboard and the aligned commentary transcript, which a general-purpose multimodal model processes jointly. Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have achieved remarkable performance in Visually Rich Document Understanding (VRDU) tasks, but their capabilities are mainly evaluated on pristine, well-structured document images. We consider content restoration from shredded fragments, a challenging VRDU setting that requires integrating visual pattern recognition with semantic reasoning under significant content discontinuities. To facilitate systematic evaluation of complex VRDU tasks, we introduce ShredBench, a benchmark supported by an automated generation pipeline that renders fragmented documents directly from Markdown. The proposed pipeline ensures evaluation validity by allowing the flexible integration of latest or unseen textual sources to prevent training data contamination. ShredBench assesses four scenarios (English, Chinese, Code, Table) with three fragmentation granularities (8, 12, 16 pieces). Empirical evaluations on state-of-the-art MLLMs reveal a significant performance gap: The method is effective on intact documents; however, once the document is shredded, restoration becomes a significant challenge, with NED dropping sharply as fragmentation increases. Our findings highlight that current MLLMs lack the fine-grained cross-modal reasoning required to bridge visual discontinuities, identifying a critical gap in robust VRDU research.

Abstract

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) successfully aligns autoregressive models in rigorous domains like mathematical reasoning and code generation. Abstract
Fine-grained visual perception remains a bottleneck during post-training of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), as perceptual errors can constrain gains from reasoning-focused optimization. Although image-caption pre-training improves perception, its influence can weaken as subsequent stages change data mixtures and objectives. Continuing caption supervision during post-training is challenging because subjective, information-dense descriptions complicate verifiable reward design. We present PerceptRL, which strengthens perception at two complementary levels. Perception RL uses a multi-agent pipeline to extract verified errors from dense image captions and convert the corresponding visual facts into QA pairs with fixed answer labels and rule-based rewards. Chain QA links correlated errors, while multiple-choice QA combines unrelated errors. A specialized route targets high-error domains with tailored verification, and a domain-agnostic general route covers errors beyond these domains. Perception Agentic RL addresses fine-grained and dense cases unresolved by single-turn Perception RL through multi-turn inspection with visual tools. We observe coordinate drift even in models trained to use tools, where incorrect crops or annotations distort evidence and propagate errors. Coordinate Injection corrects spatial operations in cold-start trajectories, while a grounding-aware process reward supervises intermediate spatial fidelity alongside final-answer correctness. Training proceeds through shared cold-start SFT, single-turn Perception RL, and multi-turn Perception Agentic RL. Across seven benchmarks under a shared tool-use protocol, PerceptRL achieves 71.6% average accuracy, surpassing the base model with the same tools and the strongest evaluated frontier VLM by 15.7 and 5.2 percentage points, respectively.

Abstract
Agents built on large language models are increasingly allowed to take consequential actions: cancelling bookings, issuing refunds, changing accounts. The rules that govern those actions, such as "obtain the customer's explicit confirmation before changing anything", are usually stated in the prompt and followed only as reliably as the model chooses to follow them. We present Concordat, a Python library that treats an agent as an object whose interface is a finite-state protocol, in the spirit of typestate and session types, and enforces that protocol on the model at run time. On every turn the model is offered only the tools that are legal in the current protocol state, calls outside that set are rejected before they have side effects, decisions reserved for humans can only be requested, and token and tool-call budgets are charged and split linearly across handoffs. Protocols are checked when they are defined, and a subclass of an agent is rejected if it could bypass its parent's protocol, a behavioural form of Liskov substitution. We evaluate a Concordat agent against the standard agent of tau-bench on its 50 airline tasks, adding simulated customers who pressure the agent to skip confirmation. The standard agent made changes without the required "yes" in 13 of 50 tasks with gpt-4.1 and in 29 of 128 task-runs with Claude Haiku 5.5; the Concordat agent made none, with no significant difference in task success. tau-bench's own reward did not detect most of these violations. Concordat is open source.

Authors: Zhuoming Liu, Jinhong Lin, Kwan Man Cheng, Lin Zhang, Shayok Bagchi, Yin Li

Abstract
Many modern vision-language models (VLMs) build on autoregressive decoding of discrete tokens. While text-based output interfaces enable scalable pretraining and strong zero-shot generalization across diverse tasks, they are poorly suited for problems that require precise continuous outputs, such as localizing temporal boundaries of events or generating robotic control actions. To address this challenge, we propose DRIFT, a general framework for adapting pretrained VLMs to continuous decoding tasks. DRIFT combines a base predictor, which provides a coarse estimate of the target output, with a generative refinement module based on flow matching that iteratively improves the prediction. This residual formulation transforms the generative modeling problem from learning a global output distribution to modeling a localized residual distribution around a strong prior, substantially simplifying optimization. We evaluate DRIFT on both perception and planning tasks, including visual grounding and robotic control. Across multiple tasks and architectures spanning MLLMs, VLAs, and WAMs, DRIFT consistently outperforms a strong set of regression- and generative-based solutions.

Abstract
Visual world models increasingly support spatial reasoning by generating RGB views of unobserved states. However, RGB has become the default interface largely because it is well suited for visual generation, rather than necessarily being the most effective representation for downstream reasoning. Different spatial questions may benefit from different representations of the same imagined state: top-down geometry can facilitate global distance comparisons, while panoramic views provide broader context for reasoning about open space. To isolate the role of representation in spatial imagination, we introduce ImagiRep, a simulator-grounded benchmark of 922 questions spanning seven question types across two settings, Viewpoint Imagination and Cross-view Integration, providing oracle RGB and six alternative representations (e.g., depth, panoramic views, and top-down geometry) of the same unobserved state. This controlled comparison reveals that even accurate imagined RGB does not guarantee accurate spatial reasoning, and different questions benefit from different representations. Similar trends hold for representations derived from world-model-estimated states. Notably, estimated RGB alone can mislead spatial reasoning, while alternative representations retain complementary benefits. Building on these findings, we introduce GEARS, a lightweight test-time strategy for adaptive representation selection, outperforming existing spatial imagination methods and showing that reasoning about unseen states requires more than generating RGB views.

Abstract
For reliable deployment, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) should recognize when an image provides insufficient evidence to answer a question and withhold unsupported answers. Yet refusal alone is not necessarily evidence of such recognition: models may refuse because of unrelated safety constraints or without identifying the missing evidence. We study how refusal on visually unanswerable questions is shaped by three sources of control: inference-time instructions, post-training, and internal activation interventions. Across three MLLMs on the MoHoBench benchmark, we systematically manipulate the meaning and placement of refusal instructions, compare supervised and preference-based post-training, and intervene on a refusal-related activation direction derived from naturally answered and refused examples. We find that refusal is highly sensitive to both the meaning and placement of instructions, with model-specific changes at the sample level that are obscured by aggregate refusal rates. Evidence-aware and general refusal instructions have stronger effects than an intentionally mismatched safety instruction. Post-training produces larger increases in refusal, but can also induce responses that combine refusal language with attempted answers. In contrast, activation intervention produces smaller yet transferable changes across prompt conditions, without evidence of indiscriminate refusal on tested visually answerable samples. These findings show that refusal is jointly shaped by external instructions, post-training, and internal representations, highlighting the need to disentangle evidence recognition from a model's learned propensity to refuse.

Abstract

Abstract
Many operations run on a budget that must rarely run out, such as a wear allowance. A wrapper keeps the remaining budget in the state, subtracts a barrier that grows as the budget shrinks and clips the combined reward from below at -m, and a standard policy-gradient learner trains unchanged. The clip level m is a price of failure risk, since the trained value splits exactly into utility before failure, the cost of a margin and the price times the risk, so whatever policy training returns earns the most such utility net of that cost among policies with no greater risk, up to its optimization error. Its risk exceeds a comparison policy's by at most order 1/m when charged costs stay bounded, and value or validation data certify it. The main theoretical result is exponential convergence of the population training flow under any parameter regularizer convex along the motions of the network's units and strong enough against the price, biased toward the prior policy. This extends mean-field convergence of policy gradients from relative entropy to weight decay, which covers continuous-time training of finite networks. Relative entropy adds noise that lets learning leave flat regions, weight decay pulls the parameters toward a reference, and action regularization bounds the risk change by the distance to a trusted rule. Experiments on an exact maintenance benchmark, a fuel-tank controller, a solvable flow and a benchmark-suite task measure the bounds' slack. We state when another method is better, without claiming this one outperforms them.

Abstract
Automated Code Review (ACR) systems integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly adopted in software development workflows, ranging from interactive assistants to autonomous agents in CI/CD pipelines. In this paper, we study how LLM-based vulnerability detection in ACR is affected by the framing effect: the tendency to let the presentation of information override its semantic content in forming judgments. We examine whether adversaries can exploit this through contextual-bias injection (crafting PR metadata to bias ACR security judgments) as a supply-chain attack vector against real-world ACR pipelines. To this end, we first conduct a large-scale exploratory study across 6 LLMs under five framing conditions, establishing the framing effect as a systematic and widespread phenomenon in LLM-based vulnerability detection. We then design a realistic and controlled experimental environment, evaluating 33 CVEs across 20 real-world projects and two popular ACR pipelines (Claude Code and CodeRabbit), to assess the susceptibility of real-world ACR pipelines to vulnerability re-introduction attacks. We employ two attack strategies: a template-based attack inspired by prior related work, and a novel LLM-assisted refinement attack. We find that template-based attacks are ineffective and may even backfire, as direct biasing attempts raise suspicions. Our refinement attack, on the other hand, is successful in 32/33 (97%) cases, exploiting a fundamental asymmetry: attackers can iteratively refine attacks against a local clone of the review pipeline, while defenders have only one chance to detect them. Overall, our findings highlight the dangers of over-relying on ACR and stress the importance of human oversight and contributor trust in the development process.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) require substantial memory, motivating low-bit weight quantization for efficient deployment. Existing vector quantization (VQ) methods often improve accuracy by increasing the effective coding dimension, which can complicate independent lookup and local codeword updates. We propose CoS-VQ (Coordinated Shared-Scale Vector Quantization), which preserves low-dimensional codewords while jointly optimizing hard indices and their shared scale across positions. The key challenge is scale-induced coupling: the optimal scale depends on the entire codeword combination and, in turn, changes candidate rankings across positions, so pointwise updates can stall even after scale re-optimization. CoS-VQ collects candidates at multiple probe scales and constructs a tangent separable upper bound for the residual-aware quadratic objective. By reducing candidate comparisons to lower envelopes of lines over positive scales, it identifies finitely many representative intervals for joint index--scale updates without exhaustively enumerating codeword combinations. On Qwen3.8-27B, CoS-VQ outperforms QTIP and LLVQ on the evaluated language modeling, coding, and reasoning benchmarks. Ablations from the same initialization further show larger gains from joint updates than from scale-only refinement, highlighting joint selection as an alternative to increasing codeword dimensionality. Abstract

Consequently, tracking and verifying historical contributions is essential for model provenance and intellectual property protection. However, existing watermarking methods are mainly designed for single-user, one-time embeddings, often fail under repeated model derivation and incremental updates. Abstract
Analog device sizing must satisfy all specifications across process, voltage, and temperature (PVT) corners to ensure tape-out success, but simulating every candidate at all corners dominates the sizing time and slows design iteration. Conventional surrogate-based optimizers search the sizing space directly, while recent large language model (LLM) agents add circuit knowledge to designs but still delegate the sizes to global optimizers. Both learn the circuit response from repeated multi-corner SPICE simulations and optimize on top of it, which becomes expensive under a limited simulation budget. Providing an agent with reusable verified designs and sizing sub-blocks analytically could save most of this budget. Combining elite-design reuse, analytic gm/Id local sizing and global refinement under full-corner verification, AnalogScout reduces power by 43.3% relative to the evolution strategy CMA-ES at an equal budget of 16k simulations on three SKY130 OTAs of increasing complexity, and reaches the final CMA-ES power after 7.0% of that budget. It also attains lower power than Bayesian optimization on all three OTAs and than two re-implemented LLM-driven flows on the two OTAs where they were tested.

Abstract
Statistical anomaly detection methods like Isolation Forest are efficient but suffer from a critical semantic gap.
