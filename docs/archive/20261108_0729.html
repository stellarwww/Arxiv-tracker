SAGE: A hybrid vision-language and geometry framework for work-zone pathway prediction under temporary traffic control
Authors: Jiaxi Liu, Keshu Wu, Weizhe Tang, Bofeng Cao, Sikai Chen, Xiaopeng Li, Steven T. Parker, Bin Ran
First: 2026-10-22T19:14:17+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-05T18:56:42+00:00
Comments: 26 pages, 9 figures, 9 tables. Project page: https://hhjj233.github.io/SAGE-page/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Temporary traffic control redirects vehicles through work zones, requiring automated vehicles to connect an interpretation of the visible devices to a continuous passing path. Geometric predictors provide several plausible paths, yet selecting the one consistent with the temporary control remains difficult. We propose SAGE (Semantic-to-Action Grounded Execution), a read-decide-execute-check framework that separates path construction, path preference, and control compliance within a shared candidate space. The central idea is to use a vision-language model's (VLM's) trajectory as a preference over intact geometric candidates. A role-decoupled generator constructs the candidates, and rank fusion combines each candidate's geometric score with its agreement with the VLM trajectory. Supervision structured around the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) trains SAGE-VLM to state the lane status and hazard side before its passage decision and trajectory. The predicted hazard side enters image-space predicates derived from MUTCD provisions, which support a final intervention bounded in score and displacement. On 2,306 real-world ROADWork validation episodes, the candidates include a path within 10 px of the human demonstration in 87% of episodes, yet selection regret accounts for 53% of the geometric ranker's error. SAGE reduces average and final displacement errors by 8.3% and 12.0% relative to the ranker and hard-constraint violations by 37%. The trajectory opinion improves all four tested generator-selector combinations. SAGE is comparable to state-of-the-art VLM modules in accuracy, with a faster-responding VLM, and outperforms four other adapted driving VLMs in displacement error and safety measures. VFlash: Fast, Accurate Visual Planning via Flash Primitive Decoding
Authors: Yu-Cheng Chou, Shihao Wang, Jin Huang, Chi-Pin Huang, Chiao-An Yang, Xianzhe Fan, Jing Wang, Zhiqi Li, Yuanguo Kuang, Ruijie Zheng, Johan Bjorck, Sifei Liu, Hang Su, Abhishek Badki, Lei Zhang, Alan Yuille, Linxi "Jim'' Fan, Jan Kautz, Zhiding Yu

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Models for physical AI tasks, such as robotics and autonomous driving, need not reason solely in language. They can use visual plans--points, bounding boxes, and trajectories--to represent spatial goals and anticipate motion. However, autoregressive vision-language models generate these plans one coordinate token at a time, making richer predictions increasingly costly. We introduce VFlash, a method for fast, accurate visual planning through Flash Primitive Decoding. VFlash exploits the known structure of visual primitives to predict their coordinates jointly, using a lightweight drafter that generates an entire primitive in one forward pass. The layout adapts to the primitive's type, dimensionality, and length, supporting compact boxes and long, variable-length trajectories. Crucially, spatial accuracy need not require reproducing an autoregressive model's exact token sequence. VFlash therefore trains on annotated primitives and directly accepts structurally valid predictions without target-token agreement. Across the evaluated 2D and 3D robot trajectory tasks, VFlash consistently reduces prediction errors relative to autoregressive decoding. Preliminary single-rollout measurements indicate approximately 25x faster generation for 3D point motion forecasting. A world action model conditioned on VFlash plans achieves 70.2% average success across four MolmoSpaces task suites, compared with 62.6% for the baseline.

Screen Before You Serve: Simulation for Production Customer Experience AI Agents at 140M Scale

Abstract
Customer experience (CX) agents use tools and large language models to address customer requests and guide conversational interactions with an organization's products. Improving these agents, especially in regulated industries, is difficult: they must detect intent, follow complex operational policies and use tools reliably. Manual end-to-end testing offers limited coverage, while live experiments expose customers to failures that can erode trust. We present a hypothesis-driven simulation workflow for screening candidate CX agents before deployment. Synthetic customers react to agent responses and simulated tool outputs enable multi-step agentic workflows without invoking production backends. We use the Snowglobe simulator on Nubank's Card Delivery agent and its expanded successor, Card Management - Nubank's highest-volume chat-support agent in Brazil. Across 4 deployed versions, simulated and production version-level binary evaluator scores show high correlation. Simulation-guided iteration increased transactional net promoter score (tNPS) by 36.69 points in a live A/B test. We also screened open-weight configurations in over 16,000 simulated conversations. In a subsequent live A/B test, the selected model increased self-service rate (SSR) by 8.82 percentage points to the highest level observed at Nubank, with no statistically significant change in tNPS. Simulation made broad exploration of models, reasoning settings, and prompts feasible without customer exposure, enabling production improvements that would have been impractical to pursue through live experimentation alone.

Edge-Assisted Crowdsensing for Explainable Cyclist Safety Perception
Authors: Hongsen Cao, Chia-Yen Chiang, Mona Jaber, Meihui Wang, Yasmin Fathy
First: 2026-11-05T18:33:58+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-05T18:33:58+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Cyclist fatality rates remain high despite policy support for active travel, and autonomous vehicles add complexity to mixed traffic. Neither crash statistics nor multi-model detection pipelines scale to continuous monitoring of perceived safety along a route. We present an edge-assisted crowdsensing system: handlebar cameras supply street-level frames, a vision-language model (VLM) turns each frame into structured traffic attributes through a single prompt, and an explainable boosting machine (EBM) combines them with OpenStreetMap road infrastructure and on-bike kinematics to predict a crowdsourced 1-9 safety score. Extraction belongs at the network edge: the fastest self-hosted model needs 2.49 s per frame and scoring takes 0.4 ms on a CPU. On 1,362 annotated frames from two London routes, the EBM regressor reaches 72.2 percent binary accuracy and a mean absolute error of 1.011 under five-fold cross-validation. It is within one accuracy point of black-box ensembles. Its per-feature shape functions give exact attribution: physical lane separation is worth +0.97 rating points, a painted lane +0.70 and a construction site -0.65. Planners can therefore price an infrastructure change in rating points, and the results enter cost-benefit analysis directly. We also report inter-annotator agreement (alpha = 0.4695), an attribute audit, and a sensitivity analysis linking attribute error to accuracy. With re-encoding, a 20 s sampling interval serves 1,000 riders at 54 Mbit/s at a cost of 1.4 accuracy points, making continuous monitoring practical.

The Power of Meta Access in the Software of the Future --- Demonstrated on LLM Chat UIs and Chat AI
Authors: Julian Kunkel, Ali Doosthosseini
First: 2026-11-05T18:22:24+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-05T18:22:24+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Chat remains the default surface for many large language model (LLM) tasks, even as agentic tools such as OpenCode excel at coding and automation. Yet the model sees the current dialogue but not the workspace around it. We explore meta access---giving the assistant a structured, permissioned view of the interface itself and propose a ladder of levels: feature tutoring; metadata, settings, and preference access; retrieval over summaries and messages, confirmed mutation, and full orchestration. Meta access can turn a chat client from many screens into one entry point for finding, navigating, organising, and operating it, while raising risks around grounding, privacy, prompt injection, and trust. General-purpose agentic harnesses are runtimes; because they can change their own settings, meta access becomes reflexive and needs a separate control-plane boundary. We ground this in the Meta Chat prototype of the Chat AI client. It builds bounded context from, e.g., summaries, settings, and relevant messages; links references to conversations, topics, and personas; supports confirmed, reversible changes; offers a browsable tutorial library; and recommends personas as user-started conversations. By exposing application state in structured form, meta access can extend screen readers from widget descriptions to semantic workspace operations. Meta access thus turns chat from a window onto one conversation into a control layer for the whole workspace, extending chat toward agentic capability without surrendering user control. As assistants become embedded in IDEs, dashboards, office suites, and operating systems, AI-era interfaces should expose a structured, permissioned model of their state and capabilities, ideally by opening their existing metamodels through a debugger-like interface.

Abstract

Abstract
Quantization reduces the memory and bandwidth demands of large language model (LLM) inference but can degrade accuracy; offloading higher-precision data can instead stall generation. Abstract
Fine-tuning large language models on narrow data with harmful content produces broadly misaligned behavior on unrelated prompts, a phenomenon known as emergent misalignment. We propose that emergent misalignment involves persona-model collapse: a deterioration of the internal representations that encode and distinguish personas, undermining the nuance and consistency of their expression. We first test this hypothesis behaviorally, by comparing how models fine-tuned on harmful data and benign controls answer questionnaires while role-playing diverse personas. Across four model families, harmful fine-tuning produces more polarized, all-or-nothing answers, suggesting more binary, less nuanced persona expression. Histograms show increased concentration at the scale endpoints, and Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence quantifies the distributional shift. We also find reduced within-persona consistency: repeated answers to the same question under the same persona become less stable. We reproduce these observations across diverse harmful datasets and questionnaires that probe different aspects of persona-conditioned behavior, while capability and simple instruction-following controls argue against a uniform loss of general ability. Finally, a mechanistic analysis of persona-conditioned activations in Qwen3-235B shows that representations elicited by different persona prompts span fewer effective dimensions in the misaligned variant than in the original and benign-control variants, most clearly in the final layers. Together, these findings provide behavioral and representational support for persona-model collapse as an aspect of emergent misalignment.

Abstract
Data engineering for large language model (LLM) reinforcement learning (RL) decides which samples to train on, when to present them, and which to batch together. Existing methods mostly rely on external feedback, such as pass rates or difficulty labels, which observes only the outcome on each sample. We argue that model internals offer richer and more model-intrinsic feedback that can guide all three decisions. We propose SAERL which organizes RL training data in the feature space of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), a mechanistic interpretability tool that decomposes model internals into sparse, interpretable features. SAE features let us view the model as a set of functional regions, each formed by features that related samples activate together. For each sample, its SAE activations indicate which regions it engages, and its gradients projected onto SAE features indicate how training on it would change these regions. Guided by this view, SAERL keeps samples with strong and non-redundant gradient signals, trains each functional region from easy to hard, and trains one functional region at a time, drawing each batch from a single activation cluster with moderate cross-cluster mixing. The resulting curriculum is constructed once with small models and transfers to larger ones. Experiments on mathematical reasoning show consistent gains across model scales and RL algorithms. For instance, on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, SAERL improves the average accuracy of GRPO by 3.1% and reaches the target accuracy with 42% fewer training steps. Further analyses reveal that ordering and batching are complementary. These results demonstrate that model internals are a powerful and practical foundation for post-training data engineering.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in biomedical and cognitive neuroscience, but their ability to reason reliably over heterogeneous knowledge graphs (KGs) remains unclear. We benchmark five LLMs (GPT-5.1, Gemini 2.0 Pro, Llama 3, Qwen, and BioGPT) across five KGs: DrugBank, NeuroSynth, Cognitive Atlas, Brain Map, and GDPKG. We introduce a multitier query dataset to evaluate reasoning depth and factual accuracy under KG-aware (graph-grounded) and KG-agnostic conditions. Inter-annotator agreement was substantial, with Cohen's kappa of 0.781 (range: 0.753-0.807) and Fleiss' kappa of 0.77. Results show that general-purpose models, particularly GPT-5.1 and Gemini 2.0 Pro, generally outperform domain-specific models on multi-hop and high-level reasoning tasks, although performance declines as query complexity increases. KG grounding reduces hallucination rates across all evaluated models. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, domain-specific models do not consistently outperform general-purpose models on specialized tasks without graph grounding. These findings highlight the value of combining broad language-model capabilities with structured domain knowledge. We provide practical guidance for selecting LLM-KG integration strategies based on task complexity and computational constraints, and discuss hybrid neurosymbolic architectures for personalized medicine, cognitive disorder diagnosis, and explainable AI.

Authors: Roko Parac, Christos Ziakas, Leo Ardon, Alessandra Russo
First: 2026-11-05T17:10:10+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-05T17:10:10+00:00

Abstract
Reward machines (RMs) have been proposed as a reward formulation in reinforcement learning (RL) to capture non-Markovian tasks where credit assignment depends on history. By representing reward functions as automata, RMs expose the task's temporal structure and allow such rewards to be handled within a Markovian framework. However, they assume a labelling function, a proposition-grounding oracle for observations, which must be manually engineered for each environment, limiting their wider applicability. In this work, we address this limitation by proposing RM-VLM, a novel approach in which a large language model (LLM) generates an RM from a natural-language task description, and a vision-language model (VLM) acts as the labelling function, grounding the RM's propositions over image-based observations during RM-guided policy learning. A VLM-based value function estimator is used to assess the agent's progress towards each RM transition and derive a dense reward signal. We evaluate RM-VLM on three domains covering non-Markovian grid-world navigation, robotic manipulation, and 3D object collection. Our experimental results show that RM-VLM consistently achieves higher task success rates than existing VLM-based reward methods across all tested domains.

Abstract

Abstract
Adaptive rounding methods such as GPTQ quantize the weights of a large language model (LLM) one at a time, propagating each rounding error to the weights not yet rounded. YAQA lowers the KL divergence from the original model with two-sided adaptive rounding under a Kronecker-factored Hessian, but its error feedback takes quartic work in the size of a square layer. In our experiments, GPTQ-2D is as accurate as YAQA and cuts the time to quantize Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU from 8.6 days to 1.6 hours. Abstract
Self-attention carries a quadratic cost in token count, which limits how far Vision Transformers can scale with input resolution. We introduce SSoG, a separable sum-of-Gaussians spatial operator that replaces dense self-attention with a learned mixture of axis-factorized Gaussian kernels, reducing the per-layer cost from $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to subquadratic in $N$. Unlike dot-product self-attention, whose Lipschitz constant is unbounded outside a compact input domain, SSoG with fixed kernels is globally Lipschitz continuous, which, via the Picard--Lindelöf theorem, guarantees that the continuous-depth variant has a unique solution on the whole integration interval, a global guarantee that dense-attention counterparts lack in general. On CIFAR-100 at $224\times224$, in a matched discrete-Transformer setting and in the continuous setting, SSoG improves Top-1 accuracy by 2.2--4.1 points across three model widths while using 8.8--16.6\% fewer parameters, with depth and training recipe held fixed. In a looped-Transformer setting, SSoG improves Top-1 accuracy by 5.4--8.1 points. We further probe inference-time scaling by evaluating on inputs up to $1024\times1024$ at matched batch size: in the continuous-depth (Neural ODE) architecture, SSoG reaches up to 154\% higher throughput and over 60\% lower peak memory than dense attention, with the gap widening as resolution grows, consistent with the difference in asymptotic cost. In the discrete and looped settings, SSoG also achieves over 30\% higher throughput than dense attention at this resolution. Together, these results establish SSoG as a practical, parameter-efficient, and theoretically grounded alternative to dense attention across discrete, weight-shared, and continuous-depth Transformer variants.

Abstract

Abstract
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is widely used in reinforcement learning due to its strong empirical performance, yet it lacks formal guarantees for policy improvement and convergence. PPO's clipped surrogate objective is motivated by a lower bound on linearization of the value function in flat geometry setting. We derive a tighter surrogate objective and introduce Fisher-Rao PPO (FR-PPO) by leveraging the Fisher-Rao (FR) geometry. Our scheme provides strong theoretical guarantees, including monotonic policy improvement. In the direct parametrization setting, we show that FR-PPO achieves sub-linear convergence, and for parametrized policies we further obtain sub-linear convergence up to the compatible function approximation error. Finally, although our primary focus is theoretical, we also demonstrate empirically that FR-PPO can be implemented as a drop-in replacement for PPO and achieves performance comparable to PPO across a range of standard reinforcement learning tasks.

Abstract
Multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) with impressive perception and understanding capabilities will enable a range of downstream applications. However, their substantial computational and memory requirements hinder the real-world implementation, particularly for resource-constrained edge devices. Quantization has emerged as an effective solution for deploying large models. While post-training quantization (PTQ) successfully retains the performance at moderate bit-width (e.g., W8A8), it suffers from severe performance degradation under low-bit quantization (e.g., W4A8), especially for small-scale models that are more suitable for edge deployment. In this paper, we propose ProQuant, a novel weight-activation quantization-aware training (QAT) framework tailored for low-bit MLLMs. First, to align the limited finetuning dataset distribution with the pretrained dataset distribution, we propose a self-relabeling (SRL) scheme to resample the responses of the open-source multimodal dataset. Second, we are the first to take a decoupled view of multi-modal (MM) understanding and language modeling abilities. We propose a progressive block activation (PBA) mechanism to prioritize the MM understanding recovery. We also introduce a vision-sensitive factor $\vecτ$ that effectively identifies the MM-critical layers. Extensive experiments across various benchmarks, covering models with 2B-8B parameters, show that ProQuant significantly outperforms existing methods. For example, our W4A8 Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct improves average accuracy by impressively 6.6% and achieves performance comparable to full precision counterparts with only approximately 1% performance drop. Abstract
Streaming video understanding requires models not only to process temporally incoming frames, but also to anticipate user intent for realistic applications such as Augmented Reality (AR) glasses. While prior streaming benchmarks evaluate temporal reasoning, none measure whether Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) can interpret or leverage human gaze signals within a streaming setting. To fill this gap, we introduce StreamGaze, the first benchmark designed to evaluate how effectively MLLMs utilize gaze for temporal and proactive reasoning in streaming videos. StreamGaze introduces gaze-guided past, present, and proactive tasks that comprehensively assess streaming video understanding. Past and present tasks require immediate responses based on observations available at query time, whereas proactive tasks require models to continuously process incoming frames and respond at the appropriate future moment. To build StreamGaze, we develop a gaze-video Question Answering (QA) generation pipeline that aligns egocentric videos with raw gaze trajectories through fixation extraction, region-specific visual prompting, and scanpath construction. This pipeline produces spatio-temporally grounded QA pairs that reflect human perceptual dynamics. Across all StreamGaze tasks, we observe substantial performance gaps between state-of-the-art MLLMs and human performance, highlighting key limitations in gaze-based temporal reasoning, intent modeling, and proactive prediction. We further provide detailed analyses of gaze prompting strategies, reasoning behaviors, and task-specific failure modes, offering insights into current limitations and directions for future research. All data and code are publicly available to support research in gaze-guided streaming video understanding.

Abstract
Medical 3D scans and reports are inherently hierarchical, with diagnostic cues concentrated in specific anatomical regions across multiple levels of organization. Yet, most medical vision-language models ignore this structure and fail to focus on clinically relevant anatomy. We introduce MedHiVE, which combines hierarchical vision-language Soft Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) with contrastive learning to jointly model fine-grained anatomy and global scan context. To our knowledge, MedHiVE is the first framework to route both vision and text tokens through a two-level SMoE hierarchy organized by anatomical scale, coupling this with report-derived anatomy-level contrastive alignment for 3D medical imaging. This replaces the direct region-text alignment of statically pooled features and vision-only hierarchical routing of prior work with dynamic, slot-based cross-modal information flow across anatomical scales. MedHiVE operates via a two-level, learnable token-routing mechanism, where local image and text features are first routed through a local SMoE and then fused by a global SMoE into unified multimodal representations. Contrastive learning aligns both anatomy-level and global vision-language representations in a unified space. Pre-trained on 25,000+ CT scans with anatomical supervision, MedHiVE consistently surpasses 11 state-of-the-art methods across 6 datasets on 3 tasks: segmentation, classification, and detection, demonstrating the importance of hierarchical modeling. The code for MedHiVE is at github.com/szczurek-lab/MedHiVE.

Abstract
Socially interactive humanoids aim to interact with humans through their bodies, adapting in real time to a partner's movement, intent, and abilities. This requires models that understand both how bodies move and what their movements mean. Yet, existing evaluation frameworks for interactive motion generation do not assess whether generated reactive motions are valid or appropriate within a shared movement vocabulary. Existing frameworks rely on kinematic metrics such as FID and beat alignment that cannot capture this semantic property, while improvised interaction datasets lack fine-grained move annotations. Inspired by computational evaluations of games such as Go and Chess, we propose Salsa as an embodied interaction evaluation domain. Salsa is interactive, dyadic, and governed by a move vocabulary and community-validated judging criteria covering timing, musicality, technique, difficulty, partnering, and originality. We present CoMPAS3D, a motion capture dataset of improvised partner salsa dance and an evaluation framework covering kinematic quality, two objective metrics (move legibility and proficiency appropriateness), and six competition-based subjective dimensions. The dataset comprises 3 hours of improvisation by 18 dancers spanning beginner, intermediate, and professional skill levels, with over 2,800 expert-annotated segments covering move types, execution errors, and stylistic elements. We define three benchmark tasks: move classification, proficiency estimation, and follower generation. Fine-tuned vision-language models perform well on ground-truth sequences and correlate with subjective expert judgments, suggesting their promise as measurement tools. Applied to Duolando and InterGen, our objective metrics reveal a large gap between ground-truth and generated dances, corroborated by expert human evaluations across six judging dimensions.

Abstract
Large language models can reason over biological knowledge, but they are not naturally equipped to work with quantitative omics measurements. Existing approaches either convert expression profiles into long textual representations, often losing quantitative information, or connect omics encoders to language models for narrow tasks. We introduce Thalamus-Reasoning, a 9B multimodal language model that represents each bulk or single-cell transcriptome as a compact continuous embedding that can be interleaved with natural-language instructions and with other samples in the same context. A key contribution is a training corpus that connects experimental measurements with biological knowledge and task supervision. We combine templates linking transcriptomic profiles to annotations and experimental outcomes, reasoning examples connecting evidence across sources, and LLM-generated supervision with task-specific quality checks. The instruction-tuning corpus spans 157 biological and general-purpose task groups. Training includes continued pretraining on 20 billion tokens and supervised fine-tuning on 5.21 million conversations. We further introduce GEO-OmicsQA, a publication-disjoint benchmark of 3,000 questions requiring models to interpret and compare expression profiles from real GEO studies. Thalamus-Reasoning achieves the best results among the evaluated models on three of four held-out annotation tasks and on all six GEO-OmicsQA variants, and the best average performance among compared methods on PerturbQA perturbation-response prediction. Ablations show a clear advantage for continuous omics representations over a text-only gene-list representation. These results show that quantitative transcriptomic measurements and biological language can be integrated within a general conversational model, enabling both predictive tasks and language-guided interpretation of experimental samples.

Abstract
World-action models adapt pretrained video generators, and vision-language-action models build on pretrained vision-language backbones; both tie policy architecture and inference cost to large transformers designed for generation rather than control. It remains unclear how much these generation-oriented choices affect control. In this work, we introduce Latent Action JEPA (La-JEPA), a compact world-action model whose policy transformer is trained from scratch in the feature space of a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 encoder. Following Fast-WAM, La-JEPA learns to predict future features jointly with actions during training and generates only actions at inference. Among policies without embodied pretraining, La-JEPA achieves state-of-the-art performance in clean and randomized RoboTwin 2.0 scenes and on LIBERO-Plus with correct instructions, while using far fewer parameters than the strongest competing policies. It runs substantially faster than Fast-WAM at comparable performance and outperforms the closest JEPA-based methods, LeapBot-WA and JEPA-WAM, on LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0 without their pretrained policy backbones. On four real-robot tasks, its average success is comparable to pi0.5 and above non-embodied-pretrained baselines. Through a design study of the choices behind La-JEPA, we show that JEPA features outperform video VAE latents, most clearly out of distribution, and that training-time future prediction choices significantly impact the policy even when future prediction is skipped at inference. We correct the LIBERO-Plus instructions, as the officially released evaluator corrupts them for most variants, and re-evaluate several strong open baselines. Without a generative pretrained policy backbone, La-JEPA is better and faster than far larger policies, suggesting that robot foundation models may benefit from architectures designed for control rather than inherited from generation.

Abstract
Tree-based speculative decoding accelerates large language model inference by verifying multiple candidate branches in parallel. However, in practical serving scenarios, similar requests repeatedly induce overlapping candidate branches. While retrieval-based methods mitigate this by reusing historical drafts, the resulting candidate trees often contain many low-quality branches as the candidate tree grows, which reduces verification efficiency and wastes target-side computation. To address this, we propose ValuSpec, a plug-and-play candidate valuation mechanism that filters low-quality branches from a supplied candidate tree before expensive target-side verification. ValuSpec introduces transfer-based supervision to align filtering with the target model's behavior. A filtering threshold is estimated from the target model's greedy trajectories on a calibration split, without any parameter training. The target model then verifies the filtered tree under the output-identical greedy rule, so accepted tokens are guaranteed to match standard autoregressive decoding exactly. Experimental results on HumanEval, GSM8K, Dolly, and SpecBench show that, in our main evaluation, ValuSpec achieves up to 2.38x end-to-end speedup in retrieval-based tree settings. Furthermore, integrating ValuSpec into the EAGLE-3 dynamic tree framework yields up to 2.78x end-to-end speedup without modifying its tree construction strategy, demonstrating that ValuSpec serves as a generally applicable and plug-and-play candidate valuation module.

Abstract
Feature reconstruction is widely used for unsupervised industrial anomaly detection in the single-class setting, where only normal images from one product category are available for training. We propose a single-class detector that asks what information one block can pass to another. We treat the depth of a frozen vision transformer as a sequence of discrete transitions. A directed predictor learns a block-to-block hidden-state transition pointwise over tokens. Adjacent-layer cosine similarity exposes four sharp fault lines in DINOv2 ViT-L, separating stable depth segments. The deepest blocks retain semantic context but lose local detail; a prediction from deep or shallow sources to sensitive targets therefore exposes the information that cannot be transported. The fault structure fixes the source and target roles, and the same rule transfers to ViT-B and RADIO-B. We use a bounded foreground prior calibrated solely on training images: it attenuates background evidence without also erasing defects outside the estimated foreground. Using one predictor architecture and a common training-only calibration protocol, image AUROC is 99.75 on MVTec-AD, 99.42 on VisA and 94.27 on Real-IAD. On Real-IAD the object-level AUROC is 96.26. At 256x256 pixel resolution, P-AUROC/P-AUPRO are 98.46/95.97 on MVTec-AD, 99.21/96.12 on VisA and 99.26/96.59 on Real-IAD.

Abstract
Current 4D occupancy world models use heavy cross-attention or deep recurrence to disentangle ego-motion from scene dynamics. This requires significant computational capacity. However, this effort is largely redundant since the ego-vehicle's intended future motion is provided by the downstream motion planner. We present FreeOcc, a lightweight continuous-time world model that introduces explicit $SE(2)$ geometric decoupling into a conditional flow-matching framework. By mathematically factoring out self-motion, we create a purely dynamic feature space. This space supports attention-free temporal fusion and allows physics-constrained ODEs to operate strictly on residual agent kinematics. To maximize deployment efficiency, FreeOcc introduces an uncertainty-aware blending mechanism. This routes well-observed voxels to a single-pass decoder, reserving the ODE solver exclusively for ambiguous, high-uncertainty regions. Requiring only 28.3M parameters-roughly 40% of comparable models-our optimal ensemble mode (FreeOcc-E) runs at 27 FPS. On nuScenes, FreeOcc achieves state-of-the-art forecasting performance with a 0.47 m Near Field Chamfer Distance (NFCD) and 32.1% mIoU. Furthermore, the model transfers zero-shot to SemanticKITTI. It also proves highly robust, retaining 92.8% of its ideal mIoU under significant sensor packet loss and timestamp jitter.

Abstract
Efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) on mobile devices requires balancing performance with compute and memory constraints. Post-training compression is an effective approach to managing this trade-off, while requiring much less computation than in-training counterparts. We propose DictLLM, a dictionary-based compression scheme in which weight matrices within grouped layers are decomposed into a shared dictionary and layer-specific sparse coefficients, directly obtained from a pre-trained model without further training. Specifically, we cast DictLLM as a four-step post-training optimization. First, we obtain shared dictionaries via singular value decomposition (SVD) of input-scaled weight matrices, amortizing rank across grouped layers; second, we sparsify the layer-specific coefficients to the target compression ratio using a dictionary-aware Hessian; and third, we refine the dictionaries given the resulting sparse coefficients. Finally, we aggressively quantize both components using factor-specific Hessian information: input covariance for the dictionaries and the dictionary-aware Hessian for the coefficients. The resulting representation reduces memory footprint by storing a single shared dictionary per layer group, along with sparse layer-specific coefficients. On language modeling and zero-shot reasoning benchmarks, DictLLM consistently outperforms existing post-training low-rank compression methods and pushes the Pareto frontier between model size and performance. DictLLM compresses the original LLaMA-7B model by 5.1x with only a 2.9-point increase in WikiText-2 perplexity, achieving a 3.6x higher compression ratio than the state-of-the-art low-rank method.

Abstract
Chasing convex function has attracted much attention in recent years, which can be formulated as a game between a learner and an adversary. In each round, the adversary reveals a convex function, and the learner selects a decision and incurs a hitting cost and a movement cost for changing its decision. The performance of the learner is measured by the \emph{competitive ratio}, which compares its cumulative cost with that of an optimal offline decision sequence. For Euclidean movement, existing upper and lower bounds for $κ$-well-conditioned functions exhibit a polynomial gap. For squared Euclidean movement, dimension-free competitive guarantees have been established under quadratic growth, but the optimal competitive ratio remains unclear. In this paper, we establish optimal dimension-free competitive ratios for convex function chasing under two types of movement costs. For Euclidean movement, we propose Clipped Proximal Descent (CPD), which combines a balanced proximal step with a clipped correction toward the current minimizer. CPD achieves an $O(κ^{1/3})$ competitive ratio for smooth convex functions satisfying quadratic growth, improving the previous $O(\sqrtκ)$ upper bound and matching the existing $Ω(κ^{1/3})$ lower bound. For squared Euclidean movement, we establish the exact optimal dimension-free competitive ratio for convex functions satisfying quadratic growth. Our result is based on a new tight competitive analysis of Regularized Online Balanced Descent (R-OBD) and a matching lower bound against randomized algorithms.

Abstract
Diet is a major determinant of health, yet accurate dietary assessment from free-living meal images remains challenging. Existing retrieval-based Vision-Language Model (VLM) approaches to nutrient estimation typically rely on textual food representations, while, to our knowledge, direct grounding on visually similar annotated meals has not been investigated. We present NutriVisRAG, a retrieval-augmented generation framework that uses a vision encoder to retrieve similar meals with known nutritional annotations and provide them as multimodal in-context exemplars for VLM nutrient estimation at inference time, without task-specific fine-tuning. We evaluate NutriVisRAG across four datasets, four VLMs, three retrieval encoders, and two prompting strategies, including free-living meals from adults with insulin-treated diabetes, where carbohydrate estimates are compared against two dietitians' annotations. Across all 96 configurations (4 datasets x 4 VLMs x 3 retrieval encoders x 2 prompting strategies), NutriVisRAG reduced the primary estimation error in 56, with improvements more frequent under whole-meal (35/48) than per-item prompting (21/48). Across the evaluated configurations, the lowest NutriVisRAG error was below the best Vanilla error in 13 of 16 VLM--dataset pairs. For GPT-5.4-mini, under whole-meal prompting, the best-performing retrieval encoder for each dataset reduced the primary error on all four datasets relative to Vanilla, with reductions of 1.7--35.7%. Overall, our results show that grounding in multimodal exemplars comprising images and respective annotations can improve nutrient estimation at inference time without task-specific fine-tuning, although the benefits remain configuration-dependent.
